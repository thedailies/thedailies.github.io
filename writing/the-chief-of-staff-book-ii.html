Manifestos

The Chief (of Staff) — Book II

To The Chief: Letters to the next torch-bearers

Ty Clark

1. Introduction

My dear Chief,

I think I need to provide a word of explanation about the document that you have just read. A handful of people have seen the manuscript since its first writing, and the near-universal reaction has been one of mystified boredom. They’ll often say it was good but extremely dense or suggest that I change it into a practical how-to schema. I feel a little like Upton Sinclair, who remarked that he had “aimed for the heart, but hit it in the stomach.”

The Chief stands in the “mirrors to princes” tradition. It is concerned with and for Chiefs of Staff. When I was about to take the Chief of Staff role, I went searching for material that would help me prepare for the role. I found little. This role is still new in the business world. There isn’t a lot of writing, let alone scholarship, on the topic so far. Most of the writing tends to be obvious and banal things: you know, you’re going to have less time than you used to? The few bits of usefulness were mostly practical in focus. No one addressed why this role exists, what its charter is, who would be ideally suited for it (or not), and how to navigate the complexities of it. Some of these things are either the things that would be passed on in oral or embodied tradition and some of them are the dark realities that often don’t get recorded, but regardless, there isn’t anything that addresses the bigger questions of this role. I aimed to do that. The Chief is a work of political philosophy, trying to answer the “why” questions while also understanding the world we live in. Political philosophy does not sell books. I am not surprised no one liked it.

Despite that volume’s lack of impact, the fact remains that if you do not have a philosophy of your role, one will be foisted upon you. (One may be foisted upon you even if you have one, but that’s a separate topic.) The best Chiefs have a sense of why they’re doing what they’re doing. Some of them are utility players, some are public relations, some are apparatchiks, but all of the best ones have a purpose that guides their actions and approach to the work. If you do not have this, you will not only be tossed about but you will also have a hard time understanding when to leave and what to go towards. The purpose of that document was to argue for why a Chief of Staff exists, what the shape of its role should look like, and how a Chief should conduct themselves as they navigate the complexities of court life. These challenges still remain.

The Chief was partly modeled after Machiavelli’s The Prince, which I had reread shortly after starting the role. The Prince, too, is often misunderstood. Machiavelli wrote during the most corrupt period of Italian politics, and his argument was more descriptive than prescriptive. He attempted to describe how people behave and suggest that a prince must understand these dark arts to rule well. Machiavelli’s desire, as he explains in his other writings, is that the nation needed a prince who could point the people and the laws towards a higher good—but that kind of prince was going to have to do some hard things to reform such a disastrous situation. Although the Chief of Staff role has no legislative force like a prince, it traffics in similarly gray areas. Wisdom is essential. Wisdom is also the thing you will be learning. You will always be operating at a disadvantage in this role. The best you can do is to become wise quickly.

Grim realities confronted carefully was the subject of the last volume. This volume, though, is not a work of political philosophy but of pastoral theology. This, my dear Chief, is written to you. It is intended to speak to the human side of this job, for it is you, the human, who is doing this role. Remember that the tools and metaphors we use shape us and our perspectives. If you define your Chief of Staff role as being only interested in the good of your team, then you will perceive every one else as either threat or thing to be conquered. If you define yourself as a Chief of Staff, then you will begin to see the rest of your life in political, statecraft terms, and you will lose yourself. This book is written not just to encourage you not to do that but also to help you not do that. We will talk about the hard balances of the Chief of Staff role, but we will do so explicitly trying to help you survive with your sanity, honor, and integrity intact. It is possible. But like anything worth doing, the forces of darkness will align against you and make it hard. Prepare yourself, endure the trials, and be reborn more glorious than before.

2. Cells, interlinked, within cells, interlinked

My dear Chief,

I regret to tell you that you will never get good signals about what it is you are doing in this role. Most people will focus on the good you can do for them: favor, information, tasks, representation. There is precious little thought about what our roles are here for, which is partly why I am writing this. It is not just to you but to others as well. I am trying to make an argument about why this role exists—a philosophical/existential claim. I am arguing for the telos of the Chief of Staff role.

Telos is an Aristotelian concept. The philosopher traced out the causes of all things in the universe, and the telos was the final cause, the animating reason/principle for why a thing exists at all. Everything has a telos, from the entire universe down to the smallest cell, a reason for its own existence, a direction it is oriented towards. What, then is the reason and direction for a Chief of Staff?

Most writing about the Chief of Staff role focuses on what the role does or the skills necessary for it—its techne—but completely ignore the “why” of the role—the telos. So too are your colleagues. Most of them will look at you for what you can do for them. Some will invest in you, the person who will be a future company leader. Few will truly care about you, the person. They will not be interested in your telos. (They are likely not interested in their own telos.) But you must figure this out, because if you do not, you will be pulled in far too many directions by people who just want to use you.

Let me be frank: your purpose here is not to be a good pair of hands. Your purpose here is not to get better at being a good pair of hands either. Your purpose is to help out your boss and the team, represent them well, and to learn the skills needed to rule skillfully. Doing things is only one part of the job. It may be a large one by time, but it is not the only thing you are here to do. More than that, you are more than a pair of hands. You are a person. Your boss picked you, the person, to be their Chief of Staff. They could’ve picked any pair of hands to help do things but they picked a person to invest in and nurture. Your boss certainly believes you are worth more; do not let others treat you as an accessory to their dreams of glory. Remember that you are valuable as an embodied soul, and that your entire person is needed.

Since you are an embodied soul and the person your boss picked to represent them and their org, one of your major duties is to conduct yourself in a manner that reflects their values and objectives. People are watching you—always. You have ceased to be faceless; from now on, people will see you as a picture of what your boss desires from them. Your conduct must be impeccable and in accordance with how your boss operates. Wherever you go, you take your boss, your organization, and your company with you. Behave with dignity, as it will reflect on all of them.

But also seek to lead from a people-first approach. You are a human just like all of us. Your boss is intimidating to most, but you know they are a human too. Do not seek to be like the intimidating figure. Everyone knows that isn’t true; they know who you were before this role. Seek to lead honestly, as a fellow human, and behave with decency towards others. You will often be asking them to do hard things. If they do not trust that you care about them, your job will be exponentially harder. An army marches on its stomach and a leader survives on the goodwill of the people they represent. You must earn that goodwill, and it starts by treating others with respect.

A key practice that will help here: wear your boss’ rank as little as possible. They all know who you are. They all also know who you were. The latter will prove more important and more enduring than the former. Do not go around saying or acting as The Chief often; this will only make people hate you. No one likes someone who has leveled past them and never lets them forget it. People will move for you because of who you’re attached to. What will make them move quicker is not you trying to impress them but you caring about their situation. The best thing you can do is to ask someone “I’m new here…can you explain this to me? What is the hardest part of your job that people don’t realize?” People will open up instantly, and you will gain both an education and an ally.

Finally, as you think about your telos as a Chief of Staff, you must look honestly at yourself. What are you good at doing or understanding? You will have unique strengths as a Chief that you bring to the role; what are they? Again, your boss picked you and not someone else, so you must leverage your skills to do this job well. A CEO had three consecutive Chiefs of Staff who were all from different backgrounds. Each was effective, although in wildly different ways, and each leveraged the things they did well and the temperament they had. If you can triangulate between the things you do well and the things that are required of you, you will find the direction your role will be headed. The balancing act is to lean into this while also trying to supplement your knowledge and become more well-rounded from your time in the role.

3. Sheep, meet wolves

My dear Chief,

A famous Christian preacher is known for saying that “the fight for joy is a fight to see clearly.” This is true for your work. This role has a fixed length for many reasons, and one of them is your own sanity. You are entering into a political arena, and after enough time in that arena, it will be hard to not view everything through that framework—both at home and work. You will need to keep connections to the outside world to ensure that you do not lose your own moorings. Remember why you are here and what you are here for.

But we cannot ignore the political and strategic parts of this role. Whether you wish to or not, you will be seeing the higher-tier game so often disconnected from the things we ostensibly are here to do. You will have to participate in the game too, and frankly, it is easier to survive if you can learn to play it on your own terms instead of trying to sit it out completely.

Playing this game requires being attentive to human nature, shrewd in understanding positioning and incentives, and careful in trusting. All of a sudden, everyone wants to be your friend, everyone has an idea they want you to hear, and everyone has something they want you to do. Who among them can you trust? You’ll be tempted to say “the honest ones,” but even honesty can be deployed strategically. The people who have nothing to gain from you are probably the easiest to trust. You will have to become wise to people quickly.

The best way to find people to trust is to be someone that they can trust. Usually this involves handing each other a dagger of sorts. Knowledge is currency, and you cannot gain rewards by simply hoarding it. You must invest it carefully, cultivating these investments in hopes of future rewards. Some Chiefs (and executives) do nothing but gossip, trying to stir the pot and see what salacious reaction they can elicit. Avoid such people; they are reefs that desire nothing but wrecking other boats. Find the people who you can honestly share information—and critique—with and be reciprocated. An ally will not have congruent desires to your own, but a strong alliance is built on mutual understanding of mutual desires. Try to build a couple of these among your peers. You will all need them. This is a stressful job, and there will be times when you need to vent to someone who can understand (safely). A strong ally will understand the dagger you are handing them and honor the trust that comes with it.

Among your org, you will be in an impossible position. People will vent to you, complain to you, and seek to get information from you. There will be no true way to have a peer among your org, even among people who would be those peers if you were not in this position. But in this situation, it is better for you to raise them up to your level of conduct. If they are complaining, you cannot. If they are venting, you cannot (to them). If they are trying to get information from you, you must be very careful. You are an example to the company, and as such, you must set that example to others. Be trustworthy. Be honest in your dealings, listening and interacting with them as a friend would, and try to point them towards a good path (ideally towards a higher good). You will have to be careful to avoid showing favoritism, but if you sense that you have an opportunity to do long-term good, invest in a person. As always, be careful in your judgments of people. Trusted people around the org can be good sounding boards.

Perhaps the hardest challenge will be the people who wish to boss you around. Your role has no defined edges, and as such, everyone will be able to invent a reason to pull you into something—and to some extent, they’re not wrong. But you will need to guard your own time for your own sake and sanity, and you’ll need to push back at times. (Be judicious in knowing how much force to use in pushing back.) Remember that you serve at the pleasure of the regent. Just as his or her concern is the entire realm, so is yours. If it concerns the kingdom, then yes, be at least aware. Ideally, try to partner with people so that you each are doing some work and it’s not just offloaded onto you. You are a very highly paid public servant; your time and attention are valuable things. Spend them on the things that most matter.

The most unfortunate part of this role is that there will be other peers of yours who view you as their extension or direct report. Where there should be companionship, there will be tension. The Chief of Staff role attracts the wrong people. Since everyone knows the prominence of the role, it attracts people who desire prominence. Since everyone knows that one attains it by gaining favor and proving oneself, it attracts flatterers and doers. The typical Chief of Staff is highly ambitious, used to directing people, and used to doing many things. They are not necessarily accustomed to thinking of their role first as representing and embodying the spirit of the organization’s leader, and they are not always willing to cede control. If you try to seek a higher good, you will frequently run up against other people’s goals. This will be frustrating—but if you’re being honest with yourself, you are also a selfish creature at heart, and admitting this to both yourself and each other is the beginning of the path forward. As much as you can, be at peace with your counterparts, but if you seek peace, prepare for war.

Because these are the dynamics of your work relationships, you will need good, trusted friends both inside and outside of work. You will not be able to tell them many things, but you will need to be able to be honest about some things to some people. We are social creatures, and we are not meant to bear these kinds of loads alone. Find and lean on people who you can trust for counsel and encouragement in this complex season of life. You will need them, and they will become precious to you.

4. What you should be learning

My dear Chief,

Your role often boils down to three major components: doing things for your leader and their org, representing your leader and their org, and learning from your leader and their org. The third component is one of the major reasons for this role: companies need new leaders, and one of the best ways to develop them is to give them a chance to see behind the curtain and slowly become familiar with that world. You should be observing, talking, and learning throughout your tenure, both from your leader and from the others you encounter.

Just as your work has tactical, strategic, and wisdom focuses to it, so too the things you will learn will fall into those categories.

The tactical knowledge will be the most evident. You’ll see what needs to be done and by whom it should be done. The proper organization of people to get things done goes a long way. Take notes about what things need to be done for a launch, a document type, or a meeting. More than that, see what kinds of things are important to stay alert to. What do leaders spend their time on or stop and focus on? You should come out of this time with a properly calibrated risk thermometer. Knowing what is a real threat, not a threat, and an existential crisis—and being able to properly communicate that to others and rally support—is an essential thing to have as instinct.

You’ll also learn how to prepare all kinds of documents and, more importantly, how to review all kinds of documents. Part of your role will be anticipating the questions your leader will ask of a document. Though you won’t have to do that as much later, you’ll still be preparing documents that go to them or to other leaders. You’ll have to anticipate the questions that they ask and the critiques they’ll have.

Also, you should learn when and how to speak. Your role will not be one that speaks a lot, but you will need to speak some times. Watch your boss and see how they conduct themselves. What do they say, to whom, and when? Perhaps more importantly, when do they let their silence speak for itself?

Finally, you should be learning who the leaders of the org are and who the next leaders of the org are as well. These will be people you’ll not just be working with but potentially recruiting to work with you. Having a good rolodex is key.

In addition to the tactical things, you should also be learning strategy. The benefit of your role is that you get a chance to observe the strategy being formed without having to make critical decisions that will shape your team. You will often be communicating these decisions outwards and watching the effects of them, so you should be alert to which things work and do not.

The basic question you should be learning is who to trust: both which individuals and as a principle. Sadly, this will be less difficult than it seems. Many people are transparent in their attempts to gain favor and power. Remember that trust is earned, not given, and that your understanding of human behavior will be critical here. What will help here is to understand who has the power in a given situation—in a room/meeting, in any organization, in your organization, in your boss’ organization. You do not need to be seeking favor obsequiously from these people, but being alert to the dynamics of the situation will help you see how people behave in light of these.

What you may be most surprised at is how much effort your leader spends organizing people. It is less direct do-this-or-that decisions and more setting up structures that will work, thinking about who should do or own something. As before, be alert to both the people who are trusted (and why) and the roles who should own certain things. Also be alert to the red flags and pitfalls that befall people, documents, and projects. Better to learn from someone else’s mistakes than to make them yourself.

Along the way, you should also be trying to better understand yourself. This is only a temporary position: you will have to transfer at the end of it, so have you learned more about what you are good at and enjoy? What kind of people do you partner well with? What types of projects are interesting to you and in your skillset? Most importantly: which people are good counselors for you? Who understands you well and gives you good advice?

But ultimately, you are here to learn wisdom, the art of living and ruling well. The larger the kingdom, the more wisdom it takes to administer it well. You will learn many things that you will never get a chance to practice but will help you navigate your own career and the fortunes of your teams. Here are a few wisdom things to keep in mind.

First, and most important: wise kings govern by a multiplicity of powers. A wise king does not just invest only in one person or strategy and does not let one person or strategy get too strong. You do not want your key people to leave, but you also don’t want them to get too powerful compared to you (selfishly) or too dominant compared to their peers (altruistically). A proper balance is needed. If one group is perceived as being the favorite, then it will breed resentment and the leader will have to spend time addressing it. Even if something makes the most sense to go to one group, it may not be wise to do it. A good leader will have their finger on the pulse of the org and know how to balance, develop, and promote their princes at the right time.

And timing is essential—in all things. There is the right time to make a play for something, the right time to make a decision, and the right time to call it quits. You will see many people who lack finesse in timing: they will push and push and push for something or will be transparent in their approach. These people will likely be annoying to you at some level. Does anyone else not realize what they are trying to do? The answer is yes, most people do, and most people are probably also annoyed by them—but some of them are less annoyed by their approach, only by their success. They will be just as willing to ply the same tactics on you. This is the challenge of ambition: you must show it to be perceived as worthy of investment, but you must do it carefully. Push too hard or too soon and you will destroy your allies and chances of advancement. Worse, the people who push hard often end up disillusioned when things do not work out. You must guard against this and develop a good sense of timing with your ambition. Wait for the moment, then go. The same is true for many meetings or movements. It will surprise you just how long of a fuse things take to emerge. The announcements that used to surprise you before are revealed to be months in the planning and socializing stages before they are even drafted. This is not bad, either: change and consensus takes time to build, and you must slowly work through these ideas by talking with trusted people over time.

Another important piece to learn is where the power centers are within an org. This is different than the people, though it may be related. When you look at an organization, what places are the most important? In a technical organization, something like the captain’s log of all connected devices is more important than the customer support tool; as a result, the org that builds that log will be more important than the one who builds the tool. As you progress outside of this space, it will be helpful to understand what components are important inside each team, as it will give you a sense of how much latitude each group is afforded.

Finally, you should gain a sense of how to chart a career. It is true that most of our lives and careers unfold for us. But only dead fish swim with the stream. And you, dear Chief, will have more opportunity to chart your career than most as a result of this position. As you leave the position, you will be able to choose your landing spot. You will also have valuable connections who know you and will be willing to think of you for important roles in the future (either here or in other companies). You should be observing how careers happen and thinking about how to navigate your own. You don’t need to have it mapped out. Just be thinking about how to be the rider and not the horse.

5. The religion of business

My dear Chief,

Everything and everyone is religious at some level—we all structure our time, attention, and purposes around what we believe are the highest goods. Even if you are not religious, you will undoubtedly have been aware of these things around you. What are a company’s mission and vision statements but simple ways of expressing its highest goods? What are leadership principles but a defined way to live well? And what is a good corporate citizen but someone who has practiced these things religiously, devoting their time to serving their company well? The shape of religious practice aligns with the shape of corporate life.

By reflecting on the intersection of religion and business, there are insights we can gain about how businesses function. First, and most simply, remember Paul Vanderklay’s maxim that “buzz words are holy words.” The things we repeat often show us what is most important. When people talk about being innovative or agile or collaborative, they are presenting something that they hold as an important good and believe you should as well. Buzz words are not limited to concepts, too; programs and groups certainly function as this. One person was known for saying a particular program name they had coined, but other people disagreed, viewing it as applying the principle too far. When leaders change and emphasize new things, the old group may push back or question why we aren’t doing a particular thing or concept that they were known for before. These are religious arguments: we had one hierarchy of importance, you have a different hierarchy, and we don’t think that’s accurate. You already know what buzz words are used often, so you know what the most important things are to each person. If you are part of a new change, you will have to think of good buzz words, good framings that help emphasize the shift.

The other major religious framework that is useful is to think of how leaders correspond to religious orders. A CEO is like a pope: they are the head of a large organization, function as the voice of God to the rest of the team, and set the vision for the organization. CEOs are not God: they can be replaced, so they are not the unchangeable constant. However, they carry massive weight, and a shift in CEO can be a seismic thing if the new pope comes from a completely different branch than the old pope. One need only look at the ripples in the Roman Catholic Church over the last thirty years to see this in practice.

The founder, though, is God to the startup. Observe any founder interacting with low-level employees and you will see mortals being filled with a mixture of reverence, jubilation, and fear in the presence of their God. The founder created the company, formed it in their own image, and imbued it with its purpose. The people who have been at a company from its early days are always mission-driven; they will be completely devoted to the vision of the founder. For this reason, a founder stepping away is the single biggest shockwave a company can endure. Changing CEOs is like changing popes, but there is no changing a founder. Their mark endures on the company far after they are gone. The challenge for a company is if a new CEO has a different approach than the founder. The adherents of the old religion will push back fiercely on this, and religious disagreements (and skirmishes) will be inevitable.

These longtime employees, then, are the company’s saints. They are the ones that the company venerates and remembers, the people just like you and me who dedicated themselves entirely to the company’s stated goods and became pillars of the tradition. A corporate saint does not have to be still employed by the company; in fact, it is easier to tell who a saint is after they have left (or when they are planning to leave). These people are the ones who live on in people’s stories year after year, whose actions shaped the behavior of others indirectly. Just as we try to emulate the saints, so too do employees try to emulate the legendary people who have come before them.

And how is this done? Through shared practices that we all participate in. I have served in a technology company that runs on an agile process. We plan our sprints every two weeks, perform them (checking in once a day), and then review what has been done and plan the next one. Many teams go out to lunch after the sprint has been completed. What is this cycle but a ritual? There is a defined order, time, and structure to the practice. We deem our sprint as sacred; a call to add something afterwards is perceived as heresy. The final review (and lunch) are community affairs that we participate in together, and the daily standup meetings function as vespers/calls to prayer. The metaphor does not need to be exact for the point to stand. There is ritual in all of life. If we are aware of this, we can begin to understand why it is powerful, why things may work or not, and why people gravitate to them.

But there is another side to the religious angle of businesses, and that is the pastoral side. My dear Chief, I will be quite honest with you: much of your job involves the care of souls. Just like a priest confronting someone in sin, there will be times where you will have to deliver a hard message. How will you do this? Do you use the same approach at all times for all people? Or do you vary your approach based on your knowledge of the other person? Since you have a deeply interpersonal role, you will need to approach these situations with intention and carefulness. Be firm but gentle. Be aware of the bruised reeds and try to avoid breaking them. Be alert to potential wolves in sheep’s clothing and oppose them. Be attuned to the needs of the people around you and address or escalate them depending on what’s needed. Be the hands and the feet of your boss, and seek to do it in a way that enhances people’s appreciation of them. Remember that you are the embodiment of your boss, their organization, and their philosophy. Your conduct must direct them to the right things. And coupled with that, remember that all of you are performing difficult jobs in stressful situations. Some of them have personal challenges that you do not know about. Some of them are still learning how to deal with the challenges that they have. Seek to encourage them. Just as your boss will (hopefully) deal more with the people than by actively deciding all things, so too should you focus more on building up the people than on directing them. Be the grace that encourages and enhances the organization by flowing through it.

The Christian faith is built around a shared meal. As a Chief of Staff, you will be part of many shared meals. If you go somewhere on business, there will be a dinner. If someone comes to visit you, there will be a dinner. If you travel with your boss, you will be grabbing meals on the go. These are all times where we draw closer together and at some level reflect on what we are doing together. I say this in the least instrumental way possible: use these meals well. Commune with the people who eat with you, and draw others into your communion. If you’re at a meal, you’ll likely be flowing in between groups (if you’re doing it well!), and you should have an eye out for who is on the outskirts. There will always be people wanting to talk to your boss; there will not necessarily be as many people looking to talk to you. Use this to your advantage. Be curious, and draw others in. This doesn’t just apply to your team but also to your enemies. Meals with them are a chance to try to build bridges, to gain some fellowship with them. They may resist or not want anything to do with you. So be it (and so remember it). But use each chance at a meal you have to intentionally move towards others and be joined to them.

The ancients often talked of “thin” places where heaven and Earth were closer. You are in a way a thin place. Your boss is the exalted one and the team is toiling below, staring up at the gap. Because you are connected to your boss but not your boss, you can bridge that gap for people in a way your boss will never be able to. Don’t be afraid to use that. As part of this role, you have and are ascending. You do not need to try any harder to prove this to others. Instead, use your role to pull others up and help them see both the bigger picture of the work world and the higher good they should be aiming for.

6. Who and why are you doing this for?

My dear Chief,

At the beginning of the movie The Departed, the mentor figure asks a potential recruit, “Do you want to be a cop? Or do you want to appear to be a cop? A lot of people just want to appear to be a cop—gun, badge, appear like they’re on TV…not many want to be a cop.” The recruit has to ask themselves what they want to do and why they want to do it. In this role, you are that recruit, and you must ask yourself why you want to do this job and what you want to do with it.

The Chief is an esteemed position, and it is understandable for Chiefs to want the job for its prestige, power, and positioning. These are clear benefits of the role. However, if you only pursue the job for its benefits, if you only seek to use the powers available to you, then you will not be as effective as you could be and you will also be failing in one of your duties as Chief.

Your boss represents a group of people, and as someone connected to them, you do as well. You both will be for them but not of them: they will never view you as “one of them” but they will also view you as an embodiment of them. As such, you have a responsibility to represent them well. You must be about their good, not just your own. Remember that you came from among them just a short while ago; they still see you as partly their own. You will not have the honor that comes from being a lateral hire. Your history can in some ways work against you in terms of respect. People will envy you in a way they will not towards your boss. You will have to earn their trust and respect, and you will do that by seeking their good first and foremost.

Culture always flows downstream from its head and source. The first few people in a startup become the culture, and the leader has an outsized impact on the culture too. If the leaders of a culture prioritize their own gain over the team’s gain, then the team will start prioritizing their own gains too. So it is for you. If you wish to help your boss, you must prioritize their goals over your own comfort. If you wish to help your boss’s team, you must prioritize the team’s goals over your own comfort. You must train yourself so that your first instinct is to do good to your leader and your team first and foremost. If you have bandwidth, first put on your own oxygen mask but then look around you to see if there are people who would be good to reach out to. Try to be alert for good work that could use a boost from you or people who could benefit from your attention/favor. Seek to use your power and influence to build up the team. In this role, you are part of the elite. Remember noblesse oblige—the responsibility to act with kindness and generosity towards the less fortunate—and aim yourself in that direction.

Coupled with this, remember where you came from. I have found it helpful to keep contact with key people from my old team. They know me well and invested in me before I could do anything for them. They are a good barometer of whether I am doing good or losing my moorings. Since the prophet is without honor in their hometown, the hometown is the one group who can be honest with you. It’s not that they have nothing to gain from you, but they do not fear you as much as others do and will thus be more upfront. If a decision is having an adverse effect on them, they will tell you. If a new policy is dumb, they will tell you. This is extremely valuable feedback. Though you must be careful not to show undue favor to your old team, you should also try to keep connections with them. It will keep you grounded, both personally and in your decision-making.

Also, seek out the next generation. Just as your boss is investing in you, invest also in them. Talk to the people who were your peers before and listen to their challenges. See if there are perspectives you can bring in from what you’re seeing and learning that can help them. Part of this is future-facing: you will be gone before long (more on that in a moment), so you will need to see who might be good replacements for you. But more than that, it’s using the gifts you’ve been given to bless others. By paying it forward, you set the pattern for others and you set up the next group to build on what you have already done. We are all standing on the shoulders of giants. Let the next generation stand on what you’ve done. Do not hoard the understanding you acquire but seek to pass it on to others.

7. Memento Mori

My dear Chief,

This is the last section of this work, and it is also the shortest. After all, the final day of creation was one of rest. It is a good practice. See if you can follow it.

In the Biblical story of creation, man is the crown of creation on the sixth day, and the seventh day is the day of rest, the last piece of perfection. The church has always kept this as a pattern: six days for all the work of the world, but the seventh is set apart for rest and worship. It is also a tacit acknowledgement that we cannot work continuously. We are created creatures—sub-creators, not the creator. We have limits. We tire. We need sleep. We need food, rest, friends, hobbies, inner renewal. We have an end date.

So too are you.

My dear Chief, you will likely be here twelve to eighteen months. Your lifespan is one trip around the sun, perhaps one and a half trips. In the grand history of your life, this is nothing. In the grand history of this company, this is nothing. Your work will come and go and be remembered no more. Who remembers the works of the Chief except the Chief alone? Even the best of Chiefs, the ones who truly make an impact, are never mentioned in the story of the company. You are a creature of dust. All the good you do passes like vapor and mist. Once it is done, it is forgotten. The next thing, the next Chief become the new focus. You are replaced.

The ancient churches often had a graveyard next to the church, not for macabre reasons but as a memento mori—“remember you will die.” A proper perspective of one’s mortality is helpful. It frames our world and thus gives the context for our actions. With the edges fixed, we can see more clearly what is significant and act accordingly. A knowledge that our time is limited makes the actions we choose have increased significance. We must choose carefully what we do.

So too it is with you.

Your time is limited in this role—which is a good thing. This role will expose you to all kinds of evils and politics. You are walking into the core of a nuclear reactor, seeing more clearly how it works but also absorbing all the radiation from it. It is corrosive to your soul. The longer you stay close to it, the more it will warp your view of the world and your approach to it. Your first obligation is to keep your soul and conscience rightly ordered, so you must be careful not to stay in this role too long.

But since you are not staying long, you must use the time well. Seek to do good in your organization. As John Wesley stated, “Do all the good you can, by all the means you can, in all the ways you can, in all the places you can, at all the times you can, to all the people you can, as long as ever you can.” In your role, you will have a chance to do much good. You must try your hardest to do this. To whom much is given, much is expected. To whom has been given an opportunity to do much good, from them will be expected much good. And remember that this does not necessarily have to be done for the org writ large (though this is good). Your leader’s directs are the next-closest to the reactor, absorbing the radiation—and they do not have the opportunity to leave like you do. Minister to them. Help them to stay human and properly grounded in the world. The company culture will flow downstream from them, so any impact you have on them will have an outsized impact on the company as a whole.

But also remember this: the next generation will not value your works. In fact, they will likely destroy many of them. You must be ready not just to see your work not be remembered, not just to see the next generation not care, but also to see the next generation undo or redo all the things you put your effort into. Many a Chief has presided over an era only to see the era shift for the next Chief and require different outputs. Seek to do things that will last, but know that the longest-lasting impact you will have is on the people you reach. The things you do, the deliverables you create…these all are mist and vapor. Prepare yourself for this.

For many of us, this is the most power we will ever have in our career. Yes, we may be able to climb high on the corporate ladder or to start our own company with us at the top, but very few of us will ever be attached to someone running a 500+ person team at a major company. This is the peak of our impact, respect, and value to others. Accept that afterwards will be a comedown. Prepare yourself for it. This is a good thing—it is quite good that people will be once again willing to tell you that they hate you or that you are being a fool. None of us should live in an echo chamber where we are blessed with massive power for too long. The goal of this role is to do good to our leader and the team, to represent them well, and to learn the skills needed to rule well. Don’t stop at the first two. Learn the third and show the world that you’ve done it.

8. Appendix: Bibliography, expanded

Leaders are readers, and you should be consuming many things, including good fiction. Here are an additional collection of books and movies that will help give perspective and mental models for you on your journey:

  1. 48 Rules Of Power by Robert Greene. The closest thing to a modern Machiavelli, Greene’s book found an initial following with hip-hop artists who were trying to negotiate with record labels. They had little formal training in deal-making but appreciated how Greene’s work pulled back the curtain on power dynamics, giving them a chance to adjust their approaches and be on more equal footing. This is very much a “wisdom” book: it’s a dark art, and you must have a good moral compass to know when and how to apply it, but you must be able to apply it, too.
  2. Dune by Frank Herbert. I read Dune for the first time in the middle of my Chief of Staff tenure and immediately recognized its applicability to my situation. Like Paul, I was a newcomer to court politics, having to learn the family trade, and trying to blend that with my own approach in a wartime. But Dune is also a warning about the dangers of charismatic leaders too. There are layers in this book, just as there are for you.
  3. Russian literature. I have read more Russian literature recently and appreciated it for two major reasons. First, the best of it has a deeply philosophical/theological note to it, forcing you to think about morality and why you do certain things or try to be a certain person. Second, the characters are often careening around a sometimes surreal situation. So too it is in life: the situations we find ourselves in are not always in our control, but we must live well in them. Reading these things helps to keep those both in mind.
  4. King Lear by William Shakespeare. The entire Shakespeare catalog is worth reading—just a magnificent mix of elaborately dense writing and great insight into the human condition—but King Lear might be the most applicable to you. It is about a king trying to hand off his kingdom, being manipulated, and dealing with the impact of those decisions. Things about kings are good reading material, and things about succession are even better ones.
  5. Ikiru by Akira Kurosawa. One of the best dramas ever about living well, Kurosawa’s film also critiques bureaucracy harshly. The ending, where the characters try to live differently but struggle, is convicting to all of us. We have been given much in this position. What are we using it for? What are you doing with the time you have in your role?
  6. The Godfather by Francis Ford Coppola. Yes, it is that good. But it also takes on a different life when you are the one in the story. The plotting seems less outlandish and more like the things you’ve heard and seen around you, the acts of violence feel less shocking and more like explicit versions of bloodless actions, and the moral decay seems less like something you see from others and more like something you hope has not taken root in your own heart. You can watch it as a textbook or you can watch it as cautionary tale, but you know it is both.
  7. Master & Commander by Peter Weir. This is unquestionably a “man’s movie” but it bears attention for two reasons. First, there is the relationship between the captain and doctor, which should seem close to something you’ve seen (or perhaps developed for yourself). But more importantly, ship stories are both quest stories and village stories: they show how a civilization interacts together but also show people chasing a goal. This is your role, these are your people, and you must deal with both halves of this story. How does Lucky Jack navigate this for himself and his crew?
  8. Pride & Prejudice by Jane Austen. It is a remarkable fact that a book from the 1800s still sounds remarkably fresh two hundred years later. The story is wonderful and astutely observes human behavior—but my goodness, the writing is superb. Read it and think about why it works so well even today. Is it the structure, the word choices, the economy of description? What can we learn from this that makes us better writers? What can we learn from the characters that makes us better humans?
  9. Beowulf, translated by Seamus Heaney. It’s a classic poem well worth reading (especially with a trusted guide who can identify the interesting items), but it’s worth reflecting on Beowulf’s character arc. He rises as a conqueror in youth, becomes a king, and then is defeated at the end. Most of his key companions scatter in his time of need. You are Wiglaf, or at least you should be Wiglaf. But you may also be Beowulf. Who or what are your Grendels?
  10. Blade Runner 2049 by Denis Villeneuve. I highly recommend Damien Walter’s breakdown of Blade Runner 2049 as a journey through the Jungian archetypes and shadow integration. Beyond that, though, it’s a thoughtful reflection on what it means to be human, how technology interacts with our own lives, and that the ultimate good is to be found by joining ourselves to a purpose outside our own desires.
