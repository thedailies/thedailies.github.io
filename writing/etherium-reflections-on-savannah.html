Etherium: Reflections on Savannah

Ty Clark

The first question to ask anyone you meet in Savannah is “Are you from around here?” Most are not. This isn’t pandemic-era escapism, either; most folks have vacationed in Savannah, fallen in love, and figured out how to move here.

The reasons are obvious: the weather is gorgeous, the eating is excellent, the architecture is spectacular, the city is walkable, and it’s not southern Southern.

The city is preserved almost too perfectly in its 1800s-era history, with wards upon wards of perfectly-maintained old buildings stretching for nearly two miles from the water. The Spanish moss—you either love or hate Spanish moss, and you better love it here—drapes towards the ground atop the live oaks, a living canopy bed for the city. There are other parts of Savannah, such as the bungalow-populated Thomas Square where I stayed, but even they are immaculate.

Savannah is the most walkable city I’ve been in by a country mile, partly due to its size (maybe three miles on the long edge) and to its squares (twenty-four mini-parks all throughout the downtown, nestled into neighborhoods). Trendy stores with Instagrammable decor and smart names blanket the streets. Even on a hot day, there will be many places to stop and take a breather. Savannah has been carefully kept in a human-friendly, human-scale state.

So why do you run into so few native sons and daughters? It’s not as if Savannah is a local magnet, either. The people you run into are from all over: New York/LA, Pennsylvania, Oregon, Canada, England, and more. There aren’t a ton of born-and-raised locals—true of every city’s downtown, but noticeably so with Savannah. The few you spot are old money types who’ve been in the social fabric forever.

The social fabric is a huge part of Savannah, past and present. Savannah is preserved partly due to a collection of women who started preservation efforts in the 1950s after the old city market was torn down. Buildings often bear the names of their former tenants, with the histories all intertwining like someone was creating a Savannah Cinematic Universe full of cameos and easter eggs. A large part of this, my bartender tells me, happens in the bars. A native Savannahian agrees. Savannah is a drinking town, and thanks to open container laws, nattily-dressed people bar-hop up and down Congress Street or go to the Plant Riverside District. The latter is a new rooftop bar created inside the old power plant (still with its stacks!) on the riverside overlooking the bridge and riverwalk. People of many nationalities, fashion senses, and alcohol levels pass through there as we await sunset. Later, it will be a loud club. Below, on the waterside, a beer garden and an open-air bar sit alongside the European-style Christmas market, itself boasting a trio of “winter warmers” stations.

All this perhaps explains the allure of the two things people most ask me about here: have you read Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil or been to Bonaventure Cemetery? A small minority of people have heard me say that yes, I have been to the Cemetery; the rest of them just wanted to tell me about it. It is, in fact, a cemetery. A large one, to be sure, with more interesting grave markers and mausoleums than you’d expect. The Jewish section (not sure whether that was designated or organic) may have as many headstones squeezed into its tiny footprint as the rest of the cemetery combined. And everywhere, there is Spanish moss, muffling the sound, filtering the light, foreboding like an open tomb. The cemetery has a distinctly Southern gothic feel during the daytime. It is not hard to imagine its feel at night. Perhaps that’s why it is twenty minutes from the downtown, close enough to be accessed in allusion or folklore but not close enough for the people to be honestly remembered.

It is no wonder that Flannery O’Connor began here. Of course, she too left and ended her life at a farm, but her formative years were spent on Charlton Street (in a very, very modest house) in the shadow of the Catholic church her family attended. She is beloved by the new natives, a perfect distillation of what they love about Savannah: old-fashioned, critically acclaimed, and dabbling in the dark underbelly of the world.

The uncomfortable moralism of O’Connor’s work is of no consequence. The luridness of her written worlds is where they stop and sip. Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil makes perfect sense as the city’s defining literary work: it’s about a historical event involving a local scandal and a who’s who of local celebrities. The book may be well-written, but it lacks the moral force of O’Connor’s interests and thus lets the readers off the hook. There is no hard self-examination needed after reading, and none performed. On we go to tomorrow’s gossip.

Savannah is matriarchal: the social circles, whispers and rumors, and presentation rule the day, not the high-powered businesses or political institutions. Indeed, what is the industry in this town besides the college and tourism? Born-and-raised Kate, who works with a group trying to build local businesses, admits that it’s been a struggle to start businesses. We met at a tech meetup I stumbled into on my first night here. The techies I meet are not the caliber I’m used to, and there’s no sharks with a clear passion for starting a business. It’s a networking event for a town that does this already by heart. They don’t need networking; they need a purpose.

All cities have a discongruity to them at their core, and Savannah’s is that it is a cocktail of old South money mixed with upper-class glamping. The purpose of Savannah, it seems, is to be a version of the South that everyone can enjoy, provided you don’t look hard enough or ask too many questions. They’re proud of how they’ve started to say more in recent years about the slavery that went on in their town, but Savannah has so much less to reckon with than, say, Charleston a mere ninety minutes away, which was the literal center of the Southern slave trade. And being the site of Sherman’s Field Order 15 which freed all the slaves gives the city some modern social score, even as the vast majority of African Americans around this gentrifying city are underclass and the college-educated whites occupy most of the restored houses.

And yet, there is still one place where your heart rejoices.

Pin Point is a small community of Gullah/Geechee on an island fifteen minutes from downtown built around the fishing and canning industries. The Gullah/Geechee people, who came from West Africa, kept an incredibly tight-knit culture together in extremely difficult circumstances for nearly a full century. Walking through the Pin Point Museum, you sense not just how the fishing and canning was done but also the life of the community itself. There is an honesty to it that there isn’t elsewhere in the region. Whether in the stories told in the documentary or the history recounted by our guide Hanif, who grew up on Pin Point, humanity, struggle, and community are more visible here than in any other place in Savannah or any other slavery exhibit. These people love each other and knew they had to love each other in order to get by. It was an intense social bond, and its legacy endures like the tattered flag that used to fly at the end of the dock. When you learn that Supreme Court Justice Clarence Thomas is from Pin Point, you both understand Pin Point’s joy and rejoice with them. He is their triumph, together.

Hanif admits that they don’t know how they’re going to keep it going. The Pin Point Museum is managed by a local group, but the land doesn’t belong to them. It’s highly doubtful the Pin Pointers would be able to scrape together enough money to acquire and preserve the land and the heritage there. Every other island and every other black settlement on the coast has been bought and turned into highly-priced housing, the kind that attracts people who like the idea of Southern living. Pin Point is the only one left.

In a city where everyone is on their best behavior because everyone knows everything, Pin Point is like a man who walks into the bar and charms everyone because he knows exactly who he is. Is there a place for them here? Probably not more than they have now, and maybe not even that.

The better question, though, is whether there will be a place for the new Savannahians in the Savannah of twenty years from now. Maybe they’ll be moved out by necessity to make way for the new thing. Maybe they’ll become another ghost story hovering under the Spanish moss. It’s hard to say. But as one famous fictional character put it while sitting on a bench in Forsyth Park, “Life’s like a box of chocolates. You never know what you’re gonna get.”
