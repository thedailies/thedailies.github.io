Product Management Manifesto

Product [Philosophy] 101

Ty Clark

Welcome to product management! This weird, evolving discipline will give you a good vantage point to see how a business works together at multiple layers. It will not always be fun but it will be a fun ride. Remember to keep your expectations realistic, your mental health prioritized, and your eyes open.

Doing a good job in product can be summarized thus:

  1. Keep the focus on the right things. This goes for you, but it also goes for the development team and the other folks you work with. There are lots of weeds to dive into; this is a job for other people. Your job is to keep people aware of the big picture and the top priorities. A clear, simple target results in a focused, calm team.
  2. Just ship the damn product. All companies (and some more than others) mostly care about shipping things quickly. Make sure you’re delivering a product—with the right feature set. Be ruthless about P1s; you won’t get time for many of them, but you will still be employed.
  3. Develop a tough skin. Product is the middle man/funnel for business, engineering, and design, which means you will represent each of them to the others and also absorb the frustration from all of them. The CTO in my org has colorfully called product “a single throat to choke,” and he’s right. Be able to separate their critique from their emotion and your performance from your emotion (but not completely; you’re not Mr. Spock).
  4. Understand the company better. Because product is the funnel point, you’ll get to see a lot of the company. You can do this job fine if you just see how your role fits into the immediate context, but you’ll do a better job if you start figuring out how things work together, how they don’t, and what everyone/everything values.
  5. Understand yourself better (and set up your next moves). Product management is usually a second career, not a first. Everyone gets into product from another discipline. It also tends to be a mini-MBA, since you get to see so much of the company. As you go through your time in product, you’ll learn about yourself, what you like to do, and what you’re good at doing. This can help you evaluate where you want to go in your career, whether that’s in product or somewhere else. (It’s also worth noting that product is a new discipline, and people are creating all kinds of variations of it, so you might be able to find something that works for you.)

What you should be learning

You’ll learn a lot during your product tenure, but here are four major things to focus on especially in your first year or so:

  1. How to manage priorities (personal, team, cross-team)
  2. How to launch devices or features (writing planning documents, managing engineering handoffs, working with GTM and support)
  3. The company’s business model (at a broad level)
  4. Understanding and navigating interpersonal dynamics (individuals, teams, offices)

What you should be reading

There are a lot of product articles and sites out there, but there are also a lot of hucksters. Avoid them. Try to get better at understanding complexity and developing your vocabulary for how the [office] world works. The one product site I consistently find worth a read is Product Mastery Now, who publishes loose transcripts of their weekly podcasts. Find an industry blog or two that look decent, but remember that they’re almost always written by either enthusiasts or brand-builders. Some things I’d suggest:

  1. Range by David Epstein
  2. The Prince by Machiavelli
  3. Angry Staff Officer (the staff officer/NCO role maps onto product management surprisingly well, and they’re hilariously blunt)
  4. The Knowledge Project
  5. EconTalk
  6. <something from smart people in domains outside Product Management>
  7. <stories of early employees at startups that scaled>

The nuts and bolts of how to do product well

Product management is a job built around wisdom. You are sub-regent, ruling a small kingdom, and it takes wisdom to rule that kingdom well. You will need to get some. Some of this will come with experience, but you won’t be able to lean on this alone (see above sections on reading/learning). This “needs wisdom/learns wisdom” dynamic will continue as you continue in this role, interacting with more and bigger kingdoms and rulers. This will come in time. For now, as you start, focus on doing the basics well—requirements, epics, stories—and start learning the things that will make your job easier.

Goal: make sure the sprint team has a clear vision of what they should be building and the knowledge to do it.

I’ve found that software teams work best when they know what they’re supposed to do in the moment and get glimpses into what’s coming next as they’re starting to wrap up their current project. You can show them more, but giving multiple projects or showing all the things coming up tends to make people internally scream. They don’t need to know everything. They need to know that you’re leading them well, and you do that by proving it in the immediate (single goal) and future (glimpses of the backlog) arenas. Also, by doing this, you won’t go crazy by having to prepare five different epics in perfect detail.

  1. Get your wikis sketched out, then start pulling in others to fill in what you don’t know. When you’re working on a feature, get the basics onto a wiki and then enlist others to fill in the details. You don’t need to have every item in the requirements to start, but you do need to think through the major items that will be needed (see if you can create a broad template for yourself if none already exists) so others can think through what’s needed with you.
    1. Don’t reinvent the wheel! Figure out if this relates to any features you’ve seen before, then leverage those requirements as a baseline.
    2. Once you have a good idea of what the product will do, start talking with design and architecture. These groups (along with your lead developers) will help you flesh out your stories and plan for any gotchas. Pull them in early, at least a release ahead of grooming.
    3. Your story needs to tell a clear story for that chunk of work; don’t hesitate to omit details if it gets in the way of the team understanding what you want them to build. If they need the details, they’ll add them in later. If they didn’t need the details, they’ll get confused and start bike shedding. The most important thing is for them to be able to understand and think through what you want them to do.
    4. Know your targets for the next two sprints, and have those stories ready and close to ready (respectively). You don’t need to have all the stories defined before pulling them on, but the “next-in-line” ones need to be ready. The ones in the sprint afterwards should be solid but in need of some details. After that, know the next 10 stories you’d like to work on, but don’t be concerned if they’re not ready yet. Be ahead of the curve, not the pencil.
    5. If no one asks a question in grooming…that may be ok. Use your judgment to tell if this is ok or if people don’t get it (your spidey-sense will often be aligned with theirs). Also, if you get the answer you need, agree to it, stop, and move on before you pull on the thread too hard.
  2. Knowing what you can compromise on and when will make you more effective. One of the hardest things in product is having to make all the low-level decisions that people don’t want to make. You probably won’t have all the info—don’t hesitate to make your questioner go get some if needed—and sometimes people will say something’s impossible because they don’t want to do it that way. On the other side, you’ll feel the pressure from your boss and the senior stakeholders to deliver the highest quality experience, without compromise. You won’t please everyone, and you’ll have to take hits BUT if you consistently deliver high-quality work on time, everyone will be ok. This means that you’ll need to know two things: 1) what can be compromised on and 2) when is a good time to compromise. For 1, develop a sense of what must be in the product, what should be in the product, and what would be nice to have in the product (and the difference between them). For 2, compromise or throw a tech debt story to your team when possible; never kill your horses. You won’t be able to do it all the time, but prove by your actions that you’re for shipping the product and not just your own objectives.
  3. Backlog management is a dark art. We’ve covered a little already about backlog management, and that’s probably about as much as we should say. We’ll also note that every feature area has a different backlog, and every PM manages their backlogs differently. Typically, you’ll have a few buckets: the active work, the next in line, the rest of the current feature/start of new feature, and everything else. As for bugs, fit the impactful ones in as quick as you can and don’t worry about the rest. Tech debt is similar but harder to fit in because it’s usually bigger. The evil truth is that you’ll have to deceive people a bit, especially with bugs, if you want to keep a backlog in a reasonable order. Again, remember the first principle of keeping the focus on the right things. You use the top of the backlog to keep people focused, but you can also use the giant haystack middle of the backlog to keep people focused too.
  4. Your calendar is not your own; use it to serve others. As a product manager, you are the official face of the team to everyone else. This means that you will be pulled into approximately five kajillion meetings to give updates on how the team is doing, what the team will work on next, gather information about something the team might work on, and much more. Your calendar will explode. Do not fight the panic; instead, block off a couple 1.5–2hr windows each week for you to do your work. When you’re in meetings, remember that you, the regent of the team, are serving in your official capacity, and that involves showing up to a lot of stuff so they can stay focused on delivery. Serve them well, both by attending status meetings and by attending meetings so you know what’s coming and can help buffer the team from outside shocks. (But seriously: do block off some time for yourself, and stick to it.)
  5. Lean on your Software Development Manager (SDM). The team will expect you to have all the answers and make all the decisions. This is not accurate; your SDM and scrum master should be helping you with some of the day to day stuff. Leverage them well—both to help you and to keep you honest. Regular syncs are good, and discuss the upcoming week, the state of the team, and more. Your SDM will be your best ally, so make sure you’re aligned.

What to be learning through the first few months. There’s a lot to absorb, so do those things first. But as you start getting more comfortable, there are four things to start learning more about (in different forms) that will help you get better in this role:

  1. Understanding more about your feature set — domain knowledge never goes out of style
  2. Learning to live in the world of already but not yet aka being accountable for delivery questions while working to define upcoming work
  3. Customer Support + Go To Market — understand what documentation is needed and who does what
  4. Start estimating how long you think features will take, compare your estimates to reality, adjust accordingly — think t-shirt sizes or releases rather than points (and account for pain, too)

Read this after your first six months — SECRET

Congratulations on making it through your first six months as a product manager! By this point, you should be internalizing the rhythms of product work, understanding your team well, and getting close to delivering a feature/device. As you get comfortable with the nuts and bolts, it’s time to start answering those nagging questions about how you’re actually doing and how your role fits into the other pieces of this organization beyond the development team.

Goal: by understanding how your job fits into the organization, you can start developing your internal compass and building your support structures.

Principle #3 for being a good product manager is having a tough skin, and that’s a shorthand for developing your internal compass to know where north is even in fierce headwinds. People will always think you’re doing something you shouldn’t be. The development team will feel you’re going too fast, your boss will think you’re not getting all the features in, the business will think you’re going too slow, etc. When this happens, you will need to have a good internal compass of whether this is legitimate critique or not, be able to enlist others to help support you, and have some trusted counselors who can help reorient your horizon.

  1. The dev team will keep you accountable but want more certainty than you can give them. You should have a coach/player relationship with your development team: you’re putting them into positions to perform, but you are not them and need to stay somewhat separate from them. The developers will always want more clarity and more control of a feature than you can give them. Do the best you can, and remember that neither they nor you can control as much as you want.
  2. Your boss will want to know a deep level of knowledge about where things are at. You know how you need to be able to project if the developers are gonna finish a feature before the release is up so you can let GTM know (more on that in a second)? That’s what your boss needs from you to report to his or her boss and so on and so on and so on. Be honest, be realistic, and be understanding. Your boss is probably screaming internally more of the day than you are because of their workload. If you’re not getting the guidance you need, that’s worth addressing, but know the difference between that and your boss asking you like a busy person does.
  3. Other Product Managers have different jobs than you and can’t be relied on as a measuring stick. Surprise! Because every Product Manager is different and has different teams with different features, all of you are more like cousins than siblings. It’s going to be really hard to judge who’s “best” or “worst.” However, it is an unspeakable rule on this team that one Product Manager is doing really well, one Product Manager is getting ground into pieces, and the rest are meh at any given time (but the Product Managers change). Support each other and learn from each other where you can. You’ll each need each other’s backs and ears from time to time. But don’t try to level yourself against others too much (except for Ann, the majestic long-suffering gazelle…sorry, some Leslie Knope just came out).
  4. Your relationship with your former team has changed forever. The philosopher L. A. Paul has a thought experiment where a vampire offers you a chance to become a vampire but you have to decide immediately. How do you make the decision? Your life will change profoundly, and you have no way of knowing what it will be like. When you switch into product, you won’t start drinking blood or staying alive forever, but you will become more out of step with your former colleagues because you are now “management.” You’ll be friends, but you’ll have a different relationship with them. It will be weird. Don’t expect to stay friends forever; instead, realize that you’ll probably develop friendships with others who are on the same path as you. I’ve ended up having some great conversations recently with someone who’s transitioning into an SDM role; both of us are starting to look at how the organization functions from similar elevation but different angles. Your background will always shape you, but you won’t always be your background.
  5. Find a couple people who can give you the big-picture view when you need it and keep you honest. Product straddles many worlds, and depending on the organization, you may spend the majority of your time doing day-to-day delivery items vs. definition, roadmap, and beyond. In that type of environment, you will need people to help you see the big picture (both for the roadmap and for the business). Find some of these people and ask them questions when needed. They’ll probably be more ok with it than you think, although they may be busy. But they’ll understand why you’re asking and be more than willing to help you. These people will be your lifelines. They will help you grow in a different way than your bosses, other PMs, SDMs, or anyone else will.

Are roadmaps…made up?

Glad you asked. Roadmaps, like much of product, is a negotiation mixed with critical path analysis. In my org, the roadmap is to some extent set for us by a strong central vision. There isn’t really a place for our PMs to go to stakeholders and say “we think this is the most important feature to work on because our scoring framework says so.” Instead, our job is to fit the different puzzle pieces into place.

The first step to our roadmap is to understand the components needed for each feature. A device needs hub work, then UI work, and those have to follow the hardware and firmware work. This is going to follow a set schedule, so we can plan for it. The same goes for all the other devices, features, and integrations.

Next, we need to plan for resources and dependencies. We can’t get everything done at once, but neither can everyone else. There’s only so many [type of] developers to do the [type of] work, and we can’t start our part of an integration until the other team finishes.

After this, the negotiation happens. What’s more important: this feature or that feature? Or, better put, can we do this feature while we’re waiting for that feature to become ready to work on or is it better to work on that other feature since it’s a quick win and gives us options? If we give one hub task to a hub developer who isn’t as familiar with it, is that a good option even if it takes them longer vs. having them continue on what he’s working on? These are hard questions, and we need product and engineering management to discuss them.

Once this is done, the head of product reviews the potential roadmap with stakeholders to make sure it’s aligned with their views. If they disagree, they also have to negotiate similarly.

It’s a long, political, unsexy process, but it results in full capacity teams doing (mostly) highest impact work. (It does not, however, get you points in your product career for doing RICE scoring or something else. If you want to do that, you’ll have to do it on the side. The exercise will be worth it.)

Goal: understand “the second sprint” and see how the product team interacts with other non-engineering teams to launch a feature.

By now, you’ve either shipped a product or are about to ship a product. Congratulations! This is a big accomplishment for anyone, but it ends up defining you more as a product manager. You are now [about to be] the product manager who did <the thing>, a marker that everyone else at the company will know. But just as you aren’t the only one to build a feature or device, you aren’t the only one to ship it. You’ll interact with a lot of people in other disciplines. It’s time to know who they are and how they intersect with you.

1. Program Management

If a project is a car, product is the driver and program is the guardrails. Their job is to keep everyone on track. They’ll ask you for updates, sometimes with questions that feel like they’re leading you. Don’t take it the wrong way. They’re trying to get information so they can tell whether they need to jump in and help. It’s a two-way street: if you develop a good relationship with your program managers by giving them accurate, trustworthy information (read: telling them accurately what they need to know, not all the drama), your projects will go smoother and you’ll be able to enlist them to help track things down for you. And trust me: program managers love helping others. They don’t always get to do it, so it’s a win-win.

You may work with a handful of program managers. In my org, the program managers are more aligned to hardware than software, so I often have to make sure they’re clear on the dependencies between hardware and software. Knowing the strengths of your program managers will help immensely.

When it’s time to beta test your feature—and that’s when, not if—you may be called on to help write the test plan, the survey questions, create metric targets, etc. Or your org might have a team that does this. Even if they do, be sure you’re in the room with them to represent your feature, answer questions about it, and learn how to do these things. (Side note: you might also need to work with a business intelligence team to build reports and dashboards for your beta/launch and customer support program managers as their teams start to build training material.)

2. Go To Market (GTM)

Go To Market is responsible for getting the device you’ve just built onto your website, into brick and mortar retailers, and for positioning the device to sell. This means that they’ll need to know a) what the thing you’ve built can do, b) when you expect it to launch, and c) how expensive it is. They’ll be working closely with you, other PMs involved in the product/feature, and the program team throughout the development process. Depending on your team structure, you might not have to do as much interaction with them as other PMs but you will have to help represent what you’ve done to them. They can be snarky; let it go. Use your tough skin and remember that your job is to ship the damn product. Even if they feel like they are fighting with you, treat them as co-belligerents and not enemies. But make sure they’re selling the product that’s been built, not the product that they wish you built.

3. Customer Support/Training and Advanced Engineering Support

These are the two groups who interact the most with users, so it is good to be friends with them. You’ll need to interact with them to ensure they know how the device works and what the pain points are, but they’ll also be a great resource for you to understand your customers better.

Customer Support and Training tends to turn over—it’s just the nature of the position—but you can usually find a handful of people who will be there for a couple years and understand the system well. If one of them is in training, that’s great, but be prepared to stay engaged with the training team throughout their content buildout. They have experience with the system, but they won’t have lived with the feature for multiple months like you have. Expect to walk through the product/feature with them a few times (mini-roadshows with the right people in the room go a long way). If you’re doing this right, you’ll get good feedback from them when they’re seeing consistent issues from customers.

AES is your bridge with the CS team. They deal with customer problems with the background of engineers, so they’re able to help you understand if something is a little issue, a big issue, or not an issue. If something starts going seriously wrong in the field, they’ll probably be the first to know and the first to figure out why it’s happening. Make sure they have the info they need to do their jobs.

Read this after your first year — TOP SECRET

Congratulations on making it to one year as a product manager! By now, you’ve got the hang of backlog management, you’ve launched a couple things, and you’re starting to see how all the pieces fit together to make a feature or device happen. You can stop here and do a very solid job at product management. But if you want to get better, you’ll have to answer the questions rattling around your head about how what you do fits into the broader world—not just the other groups you work with, but your company as a whole. How does the office work? How does the company work? What makes a team work well vs. not? Some of these questions will be answered in our last, ULTRA TOP SECRET section, but you should be starting to think about this around your one-year anniversary.

Goal: start to understand the relational aspects to work and identify how they affect you.

1. The Office

Because the product role is situated in the middle of everything, you see a lot of things. Now that the new routines have become old patterns, you can start peeling a layer deeper into what you’re seeing. As you watch the office interact, see if you can make a map of it. Can you answer these questions:

  • What does the office value?
  • Who are the key decision-makers (not necessarily the people with the titles)?
  • How does something happen around the office?
  • If something doesn’t happen, why not?
  • What are the levers that people use to get their way? Are these the levers they should be using?
  • Who is responsible for what, and are they accountable for it too?
  • Do you observe people behaving differently in public vs. private? Why might they be doing so?
  • What people have my back and in which situations?

The more you can observe and understand, the more you’ll be able to interpret what’s happening and even be able to predict what’s going to happen. I’ll also encourage you to do this with someone else who you trust. If you do this alone, you’ll be taking the red pill by yourself—something Neo doesn’t even do. This will lead to cynicism and disillusionment. Do this with a trusted guide who can help you understand the world. Remember: you are a sub-regent. This is the world we move in. But just because you move in that world does not mean you must or should be conformed to it.

Also, don’t try too hard to influence things at this point. You will get there (and will need to get there), but at this point, focus on understanding the world accurately. Remember how you’ve built up to the point where epics and stories are second nature? That’s what you’ll do here. This is the start of the journey.

2. How do I fit into how this office works?

As you start to see how the office and company in general works, you may start feeling a little puzzled. “Wait, I thought we had more control over things? I thought I had more control over things?” Truthfully, we often don’t. I’m writing this as part of a gigantic company with an inventor-in-chief at the top. I and my fellow PMs don’t have a lot of opportunities to define things or effect change the way we’d like.

This does not mean we’re doing a bad job, nor does it mean that the system is broken. Each company has a way of doing things that does in fact work. You and I may have quibbles about it and would do it differently, but our jobs are not to change things. They are to do good work in launching successful devices. Do this, and do well by others while you do it too. Be faithful in the little things. People will notice. (This is not the reason to do it, but it is a benefit.) And as you are faithful over time, your respect will grow and you’ll be able to do more or have more control over things. The best teams adapt to their best players, and product/development teams are no different.

3. How do I fit into how [my feature area] works?

My role has an interesting challenge: there are three of us (aligned to hardware, firmware, and software). In a good working arrangement (which we have), each of us is able to have and define your own areas, working together while still maintaining some control. But it is an unshakeable truth that decisions for devices flow downhill, following the pattern of hardware to firmware to software. By the time a device gets to me, it will be largely defined as a device. Our CEO will have seen it a few times, given his feedback and direction on it, and the hardware will probably be locked. I won’t be able to have as much control here or as much visibility here. (This also means I don’t have to deal with GTM, packaging, etc. as much, so it all works out.)

But I do get to define how the app works, and that is the most visible portion to our neighbors. I have nearly complete control there. And I’m backed up by a hardware and firmware team that will work to make sure you have what you need. There’s none of the “throwing it over the wall” that permeates some companies; in this org, we pull together.

So how does this relate to you? At this point, what you should be doing is understanding how the entire product process surrounding your team works. Are you the only team that touches the things that make up your feature set or are you dependent on others? Who else uses the things you make? When a change happens, how does it look? Observe and interpret as much as you can; don’t waste the opportunity (especially if you’re reporting into another product manager or working with another upstream team). If you can understand how the feature development process works, that will help you plan your releases and not be rattled by new things. It will also give you a bigger understanding of the organization and company than other Product Managers will have. And, to be honest, there will always be something undeniably cool about seeing your feature in the wild, pointing to it and saying, “THAT. I launched that.”

4. The three legs of the stool

Amazon sometimes talks about SDMs, TPMs (program), and PMs as “the three legs of a stool,” holding up the development team. This is a good metaphor, and it contains a couple important truths for you:

  • You are a leg of the stool, not all three. You aren’t able to do all three jobs, nor are you expected to. Do your job well.
  • Your SDM and TPMs are working alongside you. You are all helping to do the same job (supporting the developers). These will probably be the people you have the most kinship with.
  • If you don’t see a single person doing one of those jobs, it means the work is getting distributed elsewhere (and you’re probably doing some of it)
  • You will not be visible. If someone’s sitting on the stool, they won’t see the legs at all. This is the same for your role. Your job is more front-facing than the other two, but all three of you are primarily doing behind-the-scenes work.
  • You are essential. A stool can lose part of its seat and still work if the legs are still in place. It’s said that military groups can survive bad officers but cannot survive bad sergeants. Similarly, it’s rare to find a good team with a bad Product Manager doing good work. Your role is essential.

Read this after your 3yr — ULTRA TOP SECRET

Three years in product management! Drinks are on us whenever we meet again. If you’ve made it this far, you’ve done a lot of things and seen a lot of things. You’re probably one of the more-tenured PMs at this point, and others are looking to you for advice and opinions. You’re probably also thinking about the next steps for yourself. Is this all there is? If you want to do something different, what are the options? Is it all worth it?

You should continue to lean on the people you trust as you think through these things, but you should also lean on the experience and wisdom you’ve gained. Remember all the way back at the start of your product tenure how we said product was a wisdom-based role, that you needed wisdom to rule your kingdom well and were also going to have to develop your wisdom? You should have it, and these are the places where you should put it to use (and develop even more). Wisdom is the art of living well in a complex world. Thinking through career decisions is certainly complex, and so is managing a team inside a very large company. These are tough things. But so are you. And honestly, these are fun things to think about too. The big-picture things you geeked out about a while ago is now something you’re getting to think about and influence.

Goal: use your skills and influence to pursue good for yourself and for those depending on you.

As you consider your influence, there are two main things to think about:

1. What is your company’s business model and how do you fit into it?

This is the world that we fit into. Every product we sell is designed to make us a better proposition to a potential customer. If you can’t clearly articulate in a sentence or two how your company positions itself to make money, you will not be a good judge of whether the work you’re doing is actually bringing value to the company (and, also, your resume).

Once you can articulate that, see if there are things you can learn about the company by looking at the impacts of that. Is the company set up to prioritize R&D? Is system stability truly the most important thing to your company (or do they more care once it starts costing them more than X dollars)? What is your customers’ relationship to your company (lifestyle, impulse buy, luxury/status, mission-based)? How does all this affect your roadmap?

In my company, a mixture of aggressive timelines and low costs meant that we often ignored our P1s. Since we often just had time to do one quick cut of work, we needed to make sure the thing works. (And, since we were a subscription business, even if it didn’t entirely work, if it shipped on time and drove subscriptions, we were hitting the mark.) This meant that we needed to be comfortable with a device that isn’t perfect or even great. Aim for it, yes, but prioritize shipping the damn product—and that meant that we needed to be the ones emphasizing this for our team. You’ll have to translate your company’s strategy and its impacts into your team as well. And if your work doesn’t directly drive the bottom line for your company, well, then keep your ego squarely in check.

2. The unique place of Product Managers

Product Management is an unusual role. You are at once a regent but not with any true power. You are a generalist in a specialist’s company. You have to understand everyone, you have to communicate with everyone, you will get yelled at by everyone. You will see many things, control few of them, and sometimes see inside information flow to you (don’t let it go to your head; always consider why you’re getting the info you are).

It is also a grind. You are asked to do a lot of stuff. Not everyone thinks your job is necessary. This job will hurt you.

Three years is the usual time for an MBA, which product management is sometimes compared to. Like an MBA program, being in product management will help you see all parts of the business and how they connect. You’ll get experience directing things, like executives do, but at a smaller scale.

I think you can see where this is going: product management is good preparation for greater roles if you want it. If you don’t want it, that’s completely fine. But if you are wondering “is there more to life than this?” then yes, consider where you would like to go. Better yet: consider what you’ve learned about the company and where you’d best fit into it. Best: consider what you’ve learned about yourself and what type of job you’d be best for. As with the other things, these are discussions best had with others, not in your own head, but you should be thinking about these too. Even if you stay in product management, do you have a sense of the direction you’d like to go (individual contributor or manager)? Do you see a way to blend some skills you have with the product management discipline in a new way? Do you see a group in the company that could really use a product manager? Or do you see another company where you could learn a new part of the product management discipline?

These are hard questions! And just like the questions you get from everyone else, you don’t have to have an answer. But you should have an inkling of where to go to get more clarity. Talk with your boss, too. What are their visions for your role here?

Product management is a weird, wonderful, intense job. It’s a great chance to learn about yourself and the working world while doing some really cool things. Hopefully you’ve done all three of those. If not, there’s always next sprint…
