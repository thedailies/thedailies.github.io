The Chief (of Staff) — Book I

A Treatise On The Proper Acquisition And Use Of Skill In Governing

Ty Clark

1. On Choosing A Chief Of Staff

Organizations grow by creating more work which in turn require them to create more positions. Rare is the organization that prints money: as the revenue grows, so does the manpower needed to create the revenue and the resources needed to sustain it. The organization must scale if it is to stay functional, sometimes passing from one mode of operations into another. Dunbar’s number is a simple example of this. The human brain can only maintain relationships with 150 people, and organizations that creep towards 150 people inevitably shift from a tightknit team into a bureaucracy. Similarly, as a leader’s scope grows, their ability to manage it must also adapt. This is often done by creating additional layers of reporting, streamlining similar functions under a single leader. As more and more layers are created, the leader loses touch with the younger members of the team and they with the leader. The younger members’ ability to influence lessens, and their paths to positions of influence lengthen. A leader must be proactive if they are to retain their best talent, the ones they think can become future leaders or help transform the organization.

Creating a Chief Of Staff role is often the best way a leader can keep their top younger talent. It provides an opportunity for advancement that is outside the normal promotional path, providing a potential option for people whose paths are slow or blocked, and it is an extremely desirable position of influence. This presents challenges to a leader: because the position is so desirable, it attracts people who want its prestige and opportunity for advancement. But these are not necessarily the qualities a leader needs in the role. The easy path for a leader is to choose someone whose skills complement their own. This is why many leaders at Amazon choose Chiefs who are strong writers, as writing well is a critical skill at Amazon. A leader may also want to use this role to reward a long-tenured talent who has done good work in the bowels of an organization, demonstrating their interest in group loyalty. They may also choose to bestow the position on someone who they think has high growth potential or may be able to run a larger function. It is also a way for a leader to demonstrate their commitment to equitable hiring practices, as this is the one position that has no qualified applicants and has no tiers, and as such, the leader may be pressured to choose a diverse candidate. The leader’s choice is a complex one, and they may choose for different reasons at different times. But one thing is clear: a leader’s first choice will reverberate past that Chief’s tenure. The first person will be the template by which others are judged and by which the role is shaped. That person will also reflect on the leader: it is their choice, showing what they value, and thus their judgment will be scrutinized.

The leader must also choose from inside their own organization. It is considered bad form to choose someone from outside someone’s organization and reflects poorly on the leader’s own organization. The challenge for the leader is that they cannot develop good candidates for this position themselves but must rely on the leaders in the rest of their organization to be growing candidates. However, these leaders are not incentivized to do this; they are responsible for delivering on tactical items and are focused on developing leaders to fulfill those functions. If one of their team is named to the role, the junior leader has lost a strong member of their team, which may cause a slowdown in delivery and thus hinder their own chances for promotion.

2. On The Limits Of A Chief Of Staff

A Chief of Staff will be perceived differently by every person they interact with. Four perceptions occur most often.

The first perception is that the Chief is a second administrative assistant. This is especially understandable if the leader has not had a Chief previously or if the employee has not seen a Chief in their past work experience. However, this can also be a subtle hint that the Chief is not perceived as being useful in their position beyond fulfilling administrative functions. Some employees may plant these hints on purpose, and the Chief will have to use wisdom to interpret these. But if this is the perception, it may be a signal to the Chief and the leader that the Chief is being underused or not visible enough. If this is understood and synthesized quickly, the Chief can reposition into a stronger position. Due to the possibility of this perception, a Chief should cultivate a strong relationship with the leader’s administrative assistant and be careful to not perform all the administrative tasks for the leader. This is not just for the Chief’s sake but also ensures that the relationships are clear to others, making it easy for them to know who to ask about a given topic.

A second perception is the inverse of this: that the Chief is the same thing as the leader. Though this is less common, some people will treat the Chief as the decider, albeit one who has more feet of clay than the leader does. This misunderstands the point of the Chief. They are a proxy, not a decider. They serve on behalf of the leader but are not the one responsible for decisions. The adjudicating power rests with the leader. Also, it is important for the Chief’s own career that they not be indistinguishable from the leader; when their tenure ends, they will have to do something else, and they should be getting practice at assessing things and making their own judgments. Their best function is to be an emissary, gathering information and making smaller decisions while helping to prepare both the leader and the others to make the bigger discussions together. A Chief should be communicating what they think the leader will think or ask in the meeting as well as checking with their leader to see if there are any items that the others should prepare for.

A third perception of the Chief is that they influence the leader heavily on decisions. Most leaders have had to develop ironclad resolve to endure difficulty and have a strong internal sense of direction. The types of leaders who are considering Chiefs of Staff certainly have this. They also have likely found a partner who they lean on and discuss ideas with heavily. In technical organizations, a pairing of a business/product leader and an engineering leader is common. The two skill sets complement each other, and a strong partnership between strong leaders quickly builds a team. This partnership has been in place long before a Chief of Staff was ever conceived, as has the team they’ve built, and it will be there long after the Chief finishes their tenure. Any Chief, no matter how much history they have with the organization, will not supersede this relationship, nor should they try. A Chief is a different type of relationship altogether. It has influence and visibility into things often shared with partners and trusted senior leaders, but it will likely not be leaned on for decisions and execution like those other leaders are. The Chief should build relationships with the trusted senior leaders on their leader’s team as well as the partner, for this is where they may strengthen their influence while strengthening the organization. But a Chief should not expect to be leaned upon heavily for input into key decisions. They are a junior member of the court and are not there to have critical input.

3. On Perception

Since a Chief does not make decisions, the entirety of their effectiveness depends on influence, which issues from perception. This perception cuts both ways: a Chief must be perceptive, discerning information that the Leader can use, and is perceived by others. A leader always wants more information. Since their business is tied up in making decisions and charting where to go, they are always eager to learn additional details. But a leader is both busy and an imposing figure; people want his or her favor but are also careful how they present themselves to the leader or what they say. A leader learns to listen critically. Their first instinct may still be to engage in conversation, but they are always thinking, analyzing, and moving towards a desired outcome. As such, they have learned to somewhat distrust what they hear.

Into this world steps a Chief of Staff. This is fortuitous for the leader: the Chief offers an additional set of eyes and ears and has a completely different set of connections, giving the leader visibility into the happenings at the ground levels in his or her teams. Both of these things are exceptionally valuable and give the leader access to more useful information. The Chief of Staff will be called upon to find and filter useful information to the leader throughout their duties. Remember that the Chief is an emissary and also that the Soviet Union gifted the US embassy wall art that contained a microphone, letting them hear every word that was said inside the embassy for the better part of a decade. Statecraft does not preclude spycraft; rather, the two are often intertwined. The Chief must be able to operate in this realm, and different tactics will be required depending on the situation. However, one thing is clear: the Chief must build trust with their network, and that will mean being themselves, separate from the leader they are connected to. If the other party cannot distinguish between the Chief and the leader—perhaps if the Chief is funneling every word back to the leader immediately—then the trust will be broken and both the Chief and the leader will be worse than before. The Chief must be able to build trust, which requires a give and take, and should distinguish between times to share information with the leader explicitly and times to suggest that the leader talk to their connection. George Smiley’s protege Peter Guillam in Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy does a lot of connecting work, placing Smiley in the position to hear and interpret for himself.

As the Chief interacts with the others in the organization, they must be aware of their own perception too. Their relationship with their former colleagues has changed immediately (“the vampire problem” of L. A. Paul), and these relationships may be hard to navigate. Any time that one of two friends changes in status, the relationship is at risk; so it is with a Chief and his or her former team. Jealousy is natural, even if the Chief works to maintain the relationship. The Chief must be aware, though, that to the members of the organization they are now a figure first and a person second. This does not mean that friendships cannot emerge, but rather that others will be interacting with them as a conduit to the leader or an important person or a potential network opportunity instead of as the rank-and-file person they were. In short: trust no one except for those who are unconcerned about your status. Whenever possible, the Chief should not wear their leader’s rank and should try to blend in. This will provide a better glimpse into people’s characters, organizational dynamics, and interpersonal tensions. A Chief will never be one of the team again, but the more that they can be the meeting point between the leader above and the team beneath, the more trust they will build and the more authority they will engender.

To this end, the Chief (like the leader) must be perceived as being just. Fairness is impossible, as each person will have a different sense of it, but being broadly perceived as just and pragmatic is important. When traveling, the Chief should try to speak to key people in as many groups as possible. Even if they do not gain valuable information, they will demonstrate their care in all parts of the business and not just the ones in their past or closest to them. Similarly, a Chief may have to carefully defend someone in a private discussion if another person has an axe to grind with them, but the Chief should try to move the conversation towards neutrality instead of partiality. Though there are times when third way-ism does not work, these proclamations are best made by the leader unless the situation is completely black-and-white. The Chief can validate that someone’s feelings are happening, but they should be careful before validating that the feelings are correct. It is a bad thing to jump to a conclusion before all the facts are known, but it is especially bad if the Chief affirms something when the leader has additional information and (later) disagrees. This erodes trust between the leader, the Chief, the person responsible, the person mentioned, and the rest of the team.

The other part of perception for a Chief is how they perceive their own role. This obviously should be discussed with the leader, but a Chief should have a set of models for how they view their role. An emissary has already been mentioned, as has a filter, a spy, a sounding board, and a bridge between two worlds. A Chief should know who they are—not just what they are good at but what archetypes they embody and how their energy can be best used. They should have heroes that they look up to and friends whose characteristics they borrow and seek to emulate. These do not all have to be real; fictional characters from the arts are often useful mirrors. Although the Chief will develop in the role as they learn it, they should have a sense of their patterns before they begin so they can understand how to conduct themselves and what skills they should build.

4. On Representation

As the leader’s choice, a Chief represents what the leader wishes to publicly value in his or her organization. The Chief must uphold this. As Pericles said, “we do not imitate, for we are a model to others,” and so the Chief must be a model to others, especially younger employees. The leader functions as the head of the organization. They are the living representation of it, speak on its behalf, rule it, and experience emotions for it. Their personal feelings are secondary; though they may get angry, happy, or sad, they must often choose to be another type of emotion entirely in their official role as leader. The Chief enters this world and must calibrate themselves similarly. They must represent for and to the people their leader rules. They must understand what the organization and the leader values and model it in their conduct. It is important to note that this is not something that goes away after the Chief steps away; though they have more freedom to embody the values they wish for their new team, they will still be known and watched as a Chief emeritus and must conduct themselves with care.

But there are more subtle ways the Chief represents the leader or the people. The most basic is by being the Chief. The Chief’s presence carries with it an official authority by virtue of their role. When they enter a meeting, it is clear who is watching. A Chief can use their presence to help groups focus without saying a word (which is often preferable). Though the Chief is not the leader, that distinction is best cultivated through side interactions with individuals and not in large gatherings. In a group environment, the Chief exclusively represents something bigger than themselves.

As a Chief transitions into the role, they must guard themselves against siding too often with their old team. It will only be natural for the Chief to fall back on their old patterns, training, and familiarity in making a decision, but they must be able to focus on the duties of the new role. Their focus is no longer the good of their team but rather the thriving of the entire organization. Though a Chief is unquestionably shaped by their previous training, they must beware of publicly siding too often with their team. This extends to the amount of time a Chief spends with the parts of their leader’s organization. The Chief must be careful to not spend too much time with one group over another. However, it is important to realize that the individual who becomes a Chief does represent their heritage. It is that place, team, and culture that the Chief emerged from; they will be rightfully proud of developing the Chief. The Chief must not turn their back on their heritage, for then those people become unmoored and adrift. The best thing a Chief can do is to be engaged with all the members of that group whenever possible. The group knew the Chief when he or she was one of their own; they can keep the Chief honest and grounded. Conversely, the Chief is seeing more of the world; their re-engagement with the group brings the world closer to them and provides a vision of growth to inspire the younger staff. One never escapes their heritage, and the Chief is no exception. A healthy integration will do good to both.

5. On #doingthework

Because the Chief’s role is amorphous, everyone will have a different idea of what the Chief should be doing, how much the Chief should be doing, and what they can use the Chief for. A Chief will have to manage these ideas constantly throughout their tenure unless they wish to be running at the beck and call of everyone in the organization. To quote the late Eleanor Lummis, “there will always be people willing to waste your time.” Discernment and maneuvering will be required for the Chief.

The Chief’s work is partly strategic and partly responding to the needs of the moment. The latter always trumps the former. The nature of a Chief is to be an extra pair of hands, and those hands operate at the pleasure and the deployment of the leader. The work is often bridging the strategy and tactic levels, helping to translate the vision of the leader into the execution of a particular item. For example, projects that involve coordinating submissions from a leader’s entire organization often fall to the Chief because they have the working relationships with all of them, the alignment to the leader that allows them to operate from that level, and the bandwidth to tackle that assignment so the leader can focus on other items. The Chief is a better choice for these than the Executive Assistant because they have the business context. The leader can tell everyone to send their items to the Chief, and the Chief can collect them and build a report.

One of the challenges a Chief may encounter during their transition into the role is that the typical patterns of work do not apply to a Chief’s role. The Chief’s role is separate from the rest of the team, and thus the rhythms of the team they are used to are no longer present. There are no standups, no weekly status meetings. Instead, days are fungible, with meetings forever shifting to accommodate the new important thing. The set of meetings is completely different too. As a result, a new Chief may feel very adrift, isolated from their former team and unsure about when to work on something or what to work on. Their sense of self-discipline will need to increase: free time is often an unplanned blessing and should be taken advantage of every time. A Chief will need to figure out a balance of work and life that works for them. Free time will not necessarily be less in supply, but it will be disrupted more often. A Chief is always on call, always jumping into bigger problems, always available to answer questions from key leaders. They will have to set boundaries in order to deal with the responsibilities of life. Some Chiefs with small children routinely clock out around 6pm to eat dinner and put the children to bed before resuming around 9–10pm and working for a couple hours more. This will be a common occurrence. If the Chief’s working hours were not consumed by meetings before, they will be in this new role.

The only consistent time for doing complex work will be at the margins of the days. However: a wise Chief with a strong relationship with their leader will figure out how the Chief can carve out time in the middle of the workday for important items. The Chief does not have to be in every meeting the leader is in (just as the leader likely does not need to be in every meeting the leader is in). The Chief is responsible for managing their time to be able to get things done, and that requires all the tools at their disposal. Similarly, the Chief is responsible for managing their mental state and stress levels, and that will involve planning out when they can take time off. This is a crucial thing to learn: businesses have rhythms, and if a Chief does not take time away at the right point in the business cycle, they will be unable to take that time later when the business has swung back up, even if they are tired. Chiefs will learn to take days for recovery in this situation, but wise Chiefs take their weeks for vacation at other times. As always, good communication with the leader is essential.

6. On The Organization

Good leaders are heavily involved in the details; great leaders are heavily involved in their organizations. This is not to say that great leaders do not need to dip into details from time to time, but that their focus is best spent at a higher plane than on making individual decisions. Great leaders spend a lot of time thinking about how to organize their organization so it can work best. All organizations desire strong leaders and clear decision-making authority, but these are elusive goals. Leaders are grown over time, and decision-making ability (both individually and as a group) is a discipline that takes practice. A leader often does not have the time to invest in both simultaneously. The danger is that an organization’s decision-making ability easily atrophies back into “the leader makes all the decisions,” which helps precisely no one except the leader’s ego. If the leader is not self-aware or a narcissist, this pattern will perpetuate and become irreversible, crippling the team. The leader must act early on to hire strong leaders who can command the room by their abilities as well as their titles. If these leaders are found, the leader can trust them to run things well and make the majority of the day-to-day decisions. If this kind of group leader is not in the org, the leader will need to prioritize finding someone and then restructuring the organization around them. The leader’s goal should be to build an organization that can properly understand what are two-way door decisions and one-way door decisions, make the two-way door decisions, and raise the one-way door decisions to the proper level to be answered. If there are enough levels of that in the leader’s org, the org will run smoothly.

A leader may need to absorb some discomfort if they do not have the right people to lean on. Whether being more involved in a particular domain or having multiple types of the same role report into them instead of funneling them through a single leader, the leader will sometimes need to take on extra items so the organization can get by for a time. This will not go unnoticed; the leader will often be critiqued for this by well-intentioned people. These critiques need to be carefully parsed, though, to understand if the leader is absorbing discomfort to help the organization or making the decisions to feed their ego.

As for how many directs a leader should have, there is no set number, but leaders of product lines tend to have 12–14 reports/key parties while leaders of individual teams tend to vary more depending on the strength of their bench. A product line has a somewhat constant number of key teams that will be represented, so that number stays relatively fixed. A team, though, tends to form around its key leaders. A software team had two extremely strong mobile leaders and organized around the two of them. When one left, the remaining leader absorbed the other’s team and reorganized a single mobile guild. Other teams have moved reporting and responsibilities as team members leave or level up. At the end of the day, the goal is to put the best people in the best place to lead the team well.

A Chief will likely be in the room for some organizational discussions but will be more of an observer than a key participant. They have no skin in the game and should not desire more input than is needed. Instead, they should be taking notes (both literally and figuratively). Where the Chief can be useful is in giving perspectives of the people deeper in the organization. If a move is being discussed and the Chief knows that a key manager a couple layers down in the team will be unhappy, the Chief should mention it. This is the kind of thing the leaders will not have visibility into but will want to consider.

Perhaps the primary organization-focused item a Chief will help out with is All Hands Meetings. These meetings have a consistent structure:

  • Roughly quarterly (3–4/yr)
  • 1 hour in duration
  • Order:
    • 1min: welcome
    • 10min: business update (where have we been)
    • 10min: roadmap review (where are we going)
    • 10min: topic of importance to the whole group (1–2 per session)
    • 5min: HR updates
    • 15–20min: Q&A
  • Goodbye (w/next meeting time)

The goal of these meetings is to build the team’s sense of togetherness and morale. They accomplish this in multiple ways: by reminding the team of common goals, by reflecting on things done together, by foregrounding various people on the team, and by giving the team a chance to interact with their leaders. The Chief should coordinate the presentation, working with the individual presenters to ensure they are prepared and the IT staff to ensure things work for both the people in the room and calling in (and, if needed, recording the session for others to see). A leader should be able to be briefed the hour before, walk in, and run the meeting.

7. On Warfare

The 2012 film adaptation of Shakespeare’s Much Ado About Nothing is set in the modern day but features actors reciting the original dialogue, including their discussions about coming home from the war. It is somewhat jarring to see men in suits and ties talking about warfare, but we adjust quickly. “Corporate raiding” was part of the lexicon for an era, and “hostile takeovers” still are. Warfare is as much a part of the business world as it is a part of the outside world. War will come to every organization in time. A Chief must be ready for battle.

It is important to understand what warfare is in a business context and what the rules of engagement are. Warfare is a state of conflict, with actions taken to undermine or threaten another party. It does not have to be conducted using physical force. We speak of “culture wars,” “wars of information,” and “Cold Wars;” these are sometimes side parts of a larger conflict and sometimes the main, bloodless affair. In a business context, warfare is primarily political action, one group attempting to gain a better standing contra another. As such, it is slow, conducted in the utmost decorum and duplicity, operates in multiple theaters, and is highly strategic. Business warfare will often have multiple objectives occurring in staggered order pointing towards a major goal at the end. These goals are of course hidden, and the longer an instigating side can avoid its goals being accurately identified, the longer they will have an advantage.

Two other interesting wrinkles deserve mention here. First, since the goals of business warfare are usually something like gaining more power or responsibility, the combatants are often vying for position in the eyes of someone who is not a combatant. They are less fighting each other directly than indirectly. If it escalates into a direct conflict, there is a strong first mover advantage. Second, warfare tends to be conducted on long, slanted lines across the organization and not short, flat ones. It is uncommon for large organizations to go to war with each other. They are big enough and have seasoned enough leaders that it would be costly or inefficient for both sides. Instead, warfare tends to be towards tangential groups: a larger team trying to absorb a smaller, related team or a smaller team raiding at the heels of a larger team to take a piece they find interesting (or distract from a separate move). This means that peer relationships are important, as a strong peer network can alert people to threats or even suppress them by their very presence. We will return to relationships in a bit, but we must get to the Chief’s responsibilities in warfare.

Put simply: the Chief must remember that they are an aide de camp, not an officer and not the general. The Chief’s leader is the general. They see the board, they make the decisions, they do much of the hard talking, and they bear the responsibility for the unit. The leader’s directs are the officers. They are the ones who will be coordinating battle plans, interacting with other units, and ensuring that the flow of information to and from the top stays intact at all times. The Chief is there to be an extra hand carrying or writing messages and to learn. They will not be making bold pronouncements, they will not be helping to map out the strategy, and they will not be privy to much of the combat. This does not mean they are not important—indeed, the Chief can sometimes leverage their position in unusual ways—but that a Chief should, as ever, be watching and learning.

A wise general will assess the battlefield and its combatants. What is their size and strength? What are their strengths and weaknesses? What are the strategies they use and common tactics? What is each group trying to protect at all costs? From there, a battle plan will start to form. Wars are a series of battles and skirmishes, and these will likely be similar. Each side will feel out each other and not want to provoke in ways that open themselves up. If someone makes too brazen of a play for someone else’s team, they will be slapped down, lose some influence, and potentially be more scrutinized on their next attempt at takeover or promotion. Minimizing catastrophic downsides is a mark of wisdom and adulthood. Once the action begins, it is hard to stop and also hard to move fast. Rarely do groups get moved or reorganized all at once, and not at the first push. The rock must be slowly pushed into motion over time. This means that the defender usually has an advantage. Since it takes a long time to achieve a meaningful victory, it is likely that the instigator’s purpose will be discovered before long, enabling the defense to start anticipating the instigator’s moves. At that point, it is a question of whether response, counterattack, or delaying action are the best tactics.

Frankly, it is a good thing that a Chief is not involved in the action, because warfare offers one of the best opportunities to learn strategic thinking that a Chief will ever get, and it is easier to learn it by watching others conduct it (even poorly). Learning to assess the strengths of others, to understand their tactics and common talking points, to forecast the paths a war can take and planning accordingly, to understand the targeted usefulness of each role and deploying them properly—these are all incredibly impactful skills. They serve a leader equally well in both wartime and peacetime, as this is the kind of strategic vision needed to manage well. Even if a Chief’s leader is terrible at warfare or in a terrible spot at warfare, the experience still offers an opportunity to learn.

To learn, the Chief will need to learn self-discipline. This is warfare. People are in conflict, so we should not expect them to behave as they do in peacetime. Each organization and each level has different standards of engagement; what one thinks is respectable is of no importance, only what the top leader tolerates. And getting annoyed with nakedly political speeches is pointless; those speeches have a purpose, and the Chief should be able to figure out what that is (even if it’s just getting under an opponent’s skin). A Chief will need to be extremely alert and extremely thoughtful during warfare, and one cannot be alert and thoughtful if one is angry or huddled in a foxhole. Self-mastery is a non-negotiable.

And, as a Chief progresses in warfare, they will likely begin seeing the field differently as the war progresses. They may even see places that would be a good area for them to move into once their time as Chief is over—either places inside their organization or ones in an organization that can be captured. This is a mark of understanding. A Chief will need to take care how they maneuver to those positions, but warfare and the realignments that happen from it may provide new opportunities for the leader, the Chief, and the organization.

8. On Changing

A man grows from a priest into a king and then into a prophet. At first, he follows the procedures required of a priest, learning the rules and being formed by them. Then, when he has come of age and strength, he becomes a king, using both his intellect and his strength to rule a kingdom well. Finally, when his strength is gone, he becomes a prophet, using his words and influence to direct and instruct the next generation of kings.

A person enters into a Chief of Staff role as a priest and, ideally, leaves as a king. However, they do not practice the art of being a king. That role belongs to the king. Instead, they function as a priest working directly with a king, learning by observing. The work they do is priestly, but it is a level higher than they are used to. A king may command the troops on the battlefield, but he is not interested in the individual movements of the troops. That role is filled by a captain or sergeant—a priestly role a Chief played prior to becoming the Chief. It may be hard for a new Chief to change the patterns they have learned in their prior role; after all, they have been named a Chief because they have done well in a prior role. But if a Chief does not learn to identify the new patterns they are to emulate and start practicing them quickly, they will be a placeholder for the next Chief of Staff and will not progress in their career.

The Chief should be learning the skills needed to lead a group of people. By the time they become a Chief, they have likely learned how to manage a group, directing them and staying on top of them to achieve a goal. But leading them is a new layer, requiring vision and motivation as well as the key component of this role: wisdom. The things that a good leader says will not often be dramatic visions from heaven. They will most often seem mundane or “anyone could have thought of that” items. However, the reason the leader is there is because they know which one of the fifty things is the right one to focus on at that point. They have internalized the rhythms of business and have a sense of what to focus on. This, above all, is what the Chief should be learning—and it presents an interesting challenge for the Chief. As the hand of the king, the Chief will often be asked questions about what the leader would think or do about a particular topic, but the Chief does not have the lived experience that the leader does. Their sense of what’s important will be different, especially at the start of a tenure. Given this, the Chief should be slow to speak until they have observed and absorbed enough of the leader to understand their decision making. If the Chief does have to make an answer early on, they should use questions to help both them and the questioner think through the situation and try to come to an answer. If they can’t, the question goes to the leader.

If pursuing wisdom and learning to lead well are the key goals of The Chief’s tenure, then they cannot only learn from one leader. The Chief should be a student of leaders, observing them carefully and interacting with other fellow young leaders. A Chief should try to become friends with not just the other Chiefs in other organizations but also with young leaders in parts of their organization. This group should become a loose “study group,” discussing the things they are seeing in meetings and trying to integrate them into their own models of leadership. Ideally, these leaders should be in types of roles the Chief is weaker in, giving the Chief an opportunity to ask questions and learn easily. If the Chief builds these relationships well, they can become a network of trust that the Chief can use to get or confirm information as well. Cultivating these relationships will take carefulness—the Chief should beware of appearing too close to any group or groups due to his or her position near the leader—but will have many rewards.

However, as a Chief starts interacting more with leaders and less with the on-the-ground teams, the Chief will themselves change. Their perspective will naturally shift from an understanding of where things are at to where they should go, going from the tactical to the strategic. The more they shift in this direction, the further they will grow from their old life and friendships. This is the Vampire Problem of L. A. Paul: if you are offered the chance to become a vampire but must make the decision on the spot, how can you possibly choose well, knowing that reality will irrevocably alter on the other side of that decision but not knowing what that reality will look like? Becoming a Chief is similar; there is no “going back to your farm” once your tour is over. The Chief will have changed, and they will have joined a different class. None of this is to say that the Chief should sever their ties with their old groups; on the contrary, each needs the other to grow. The old group will grow from the Chief relaying and contextualizing information, and the Chief will get information and see how decisions impact the ground level. But the Chief cannot go back. As such, the role of the Chief should be entered into with care. It is best for people who realize that they are at the point of changing but don’t know what the next role should be. It is often chosen by people who want to change into a leader overnight. Neither group realizes how much the role will change them, though, and that it will reveal as many things as it shapes.

9. On Wisdom

Wisdom is the province of kings. By it, they rule well, making difficult decisions for the good of their people. A wise man is not the same as a learned man. Many of us have known educated fools and wise laborers. Wisdom, then, must be learned as an art. While principles can be discerned and imbibed, true wisdom only comes with the experience to know what is important and when. Knowledge is the first stage, then understanding, and finally wisdom. The Chief and the leader are in two different stages. The leader operates from wisdom of the business and strategy while the Chief operates from an understanding level of the business (hopefully) and a knowledge level of strategy. This presents two challenges. First, the leader and the Chief are operating on the same things at vastly different levels. This will cause progress to be slow, especially in the early stages of their relationship. Each will have to move towards the other. But the other challenge is that the Chief will be required to operate with wisdom at times, standing in for the leader or forecasting what the leader would say. As a result, the first thing they need to develop wisdom for is their leader. If the Chief cannot quickly develop a sense of what their leader would say or do in a given situation, the leader’s teams will quickly decide the Chief is not useful to them and go straight to the leader. Now the leader has not only gotten no benefit from adding a Chief but they also will have to explain things to the Chief. A Chief must make every effort to learn their leader in their first few months.

The other area that the Chief will likely have to develop wisdom in quickly is regarding resource management. Many leaders delegate the control of their headcount to their Chief. Giving the OK for job postings is a task that needs to be done with carefulness and wisdom, and only the leader sees enough of the organization to assess if a position should be greenlit, rejected, or if its spot should be given to another team instead. But this task, though strategic, is also rote, and the leader does not need to manage the pool of available roles or money they have. They need someone they trust to hand it off to, and the Chief is ideally positioned for this. The Chief sees what the leader does, so they know the business, but they can also absorb the overhead of this more effectively than the leader can. Most Chiefs will work with the leader to know what their sense of hiring is, then manage the list, doling out positions as needed to the teams who ask. This introduces another wisdom component: not all teams will ask in the same way. Some teams are more persistent than others, and the Chief will have to use interpersonal skill to assess and respond to each request. They will need to maintain good relationships with the team leaders, but they also must be able to hold the line. This is good practice for the Chief to develop this blend of gentleness and firmness, which they will need to exercise in the future. It is also good practice for interpersonal skill. Some team managers will prefer (and be able to handle) receiving their entire allocation all at once, while some should only get allocations one at a time. It will be the Chief’s job to assess what the right method is and use it.

The Chief will also likely be heavily involved in the yearly planning processes. They will likely not be consulted on the plans but will be needed to document the plans and edit them, as writing is an essential skill for a Chief. If the leader has an offsite planning event, the Chief will need to help plan it out and coordinate the logistics before, during, and after. When the entire organization is working on the all-encompassing plan, the Chief will often be a point of contact or the proxy, listening to see what is said or needed.

All these things will help a Chief gain understanding and wisdom for managing a team inside the organization they are serving. But if a Chief stops here, their vision of leadership and their sense of wisdom will be incomplete. A Chief must be well-read, consuming things outside their domain. This applies internally and externally. The Chief should be forever curious about other parts of the business and should leverage their natural relationships with other Chiefs to learn more about how the rest of the business operates. The Chief should also be reading about other companies, other fields, and other hobbies, going deep into as many as they can. The more mental models they have, the more things they will be able to draw from when dealing with a challenge. The shape of an airplane wing was modeled after a bird’s wing, despite the bird not being a mechanical object. Why? Because the designers drew from related fields and were able to find concepts that translated. Similarly, leadership lessons can be learned from multiple fields and contexts. The more of these that a leader knows, the quicker they’ll be able to spot the differences, size up which items work well in each context, and develop a style of their own.

The Chief should be observing other leaders as well. Though they will undoubtedly be influenced heavily by the leader they are supporting, they should be watching leaders in other domains. Groups that have more than one leader are especially worth watching; how does a country with a sovereign and a prime minister function as a government? The more examples a Chief can internalize—especially if they are able to watch them in person—the more they will build a strong sense of leadership.

10. On Succession

As all things come to an end sometime, so too does the Chief’s tenure. A few short months and the door closes, only to reopen for a new tenant. The chapter is done. It is time for all to move forward. A peaceful transfer of power is one of the unique gifts to the world of a democratic form of government, and the organization is well served if the Chief’s transition follows this pattern. Like a dinner guest, a Chief should not overstay their welcome. However, they can leave the role in a good spot for their replacement and help to identify them.

Before the Chief leaves, there are three important documents to have ready and pass on. The first is an org chart for the leader showing both functional areas and key leaders. Not every leader has a perfectly hierarchical org chart. Many have leaders overseeing multiple groups or building frameworks used by teams inside and outside the org. Looking at the Phone Tool will give part of a perspective but not the entire perspective. A separate document showing how the work is organized is immensely useful for the entire org and should be maintained at all times. Names are optional; they will help, but the structure is much more important (and will often hint at the names). This document will help anyone understand the leader’s organization, including an incoming Chief.

The second document that is important is a list of the organization’s key goals and projects (preferably with status). A Chief will often be responsible for keeping track of these and updating the status of the highest-level goals, and it will be good for them to see what projects are underway and what goals the team is working towards.

Finally, the Chief should be given a copy of the previous year’s OP1 document so they can see what the team has been working on. Even if the team does not feature prominently in the document and even if the new Chief does not know all the things being discussed, it will still provide the Chief with a sense of what the team cares about and is working on. Chiefs are often a major part of the OP1 document process, so it will be important for them to get as much context as they can find here.

If the Chief is included in the search process, they should be honest if not transparent with the prospective Chiefs. The Chief does not need to volunteer vast amounts of information. In some respects, it is better if they are quieter and observe the questions that the prospective Chiefs ask. This will give them a window into the prospect’s thought process and curiosity. A Chief will likely be most useful in compiling the list of prospective Chiefs. They understand the job the best of anyone and also have a better sense of the future leaders of the company than the leader might. A Chief should have long and short lists of potential replacements ready at any point, and the Chief should be carefully keeping tabs on these people. It is important that the Chief pull from across the organization in this list and not lean upon their roots. A Chief is a unique, valuable position, and it is important to not have one team monopolize it, no matter how good that team might be.

Part of the reason for the Chief needing to have a list at all times is that the Chief needs to be thinking through their next moves as well. The Chief should be aware of projects happening around the leader’s org (and other orgs) and be willing to make a move if the right opportunity comes along, even if the Chief has not hit the end of their tenure. If the right fit emerges, it is worth pursuing. The nature of the Chief is cyclical and transient; leaving is always the plan. If a great opportunity to leave comes along, it is worth the Chief leaving. A Chief leaving early does multiple good things: it highlights the new opportunity, drawing attention to its desirability (potentially attracting others); it draws attention to the vacant Chief position, drawing attention from new candidates; and, if the position works out, it brings honor to the Chief and the leader who selected them for finding and developing a good candidate. To this end, a Chief should always be as aware as possible about projects that are emerging.

However, if nothing emerges by the time the Chief should depart, the Chief must keep in mind that their choice will be watched carefully. Wherever they choose to go will automatically be treated with higher honor, as people will perceive the blessing of the leader upon that group or suspect that the Chief will maintain their direct line to the leader and pass on any path-crossing or gossip from the team. The Chief has ceased to be a normal employee. They will now forever be viewed as management, no matter how many levels are between them and the leader. By moving farther away from the leader, they will be farther from the protection, however, and they will need to pick a good spot and then use the skills they have learned if they are to thrive in their new location.
