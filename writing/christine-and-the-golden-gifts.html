Christine and the Golden Gifts

Ty Clark

There once was a young maiden named Christine whose fingers could spin words into gold. Although she had been born with the gift, it did not manifest at an early age. Christine loved to run through the fields, laughing over her shoulder at all the people who chased her, charmed by the sound of her voice. “She must be a forest fairy lost in the plains,” said the townspeople. “But who ever heard of a forest fairy in Riverwall?”

Though all the townspeople loved Christine and she loved them, she felt a special kinship with the storytellers. She would sit in rapt attention as the old men spun stories of giants and princes, maidens and queens, of daring adventures and sweeping romances. She would feel transported into the world, only rediscovering her home lands when the story ended.

The storytellers loved her too, for they understood that in any tale there are three characters: the teller, the tale, and the listener, and all three must work together. Christine was a great listener, engaged and inquisitive, always in tune with the rhythms of the story and helping to draw them out with a question, a widened eye, or a peal of laughter.

Christine learned to write as the old storytellers began passing away. She tried to write down their stories and preserve them, but she was not yet skilled in these arts.

One day, when she was trying to write down the story of the oldest storyteller, her hand caught a splinter in the wooden table, and she recoiled in pain. The splinter made it hard to concentrate on the words, and she scarcely finished a page before stopping. As she folded up the page, a thin strand of gold fell out and onto the table. Christine had not seen it form, but the words she wrote had turned into gold—only a little, but still a thread of gold.

The next day, Christine wrote another page, trying to see if the gold would appear again, but it would not. Another page she wrote, but still the gold did not appear. Warily, and with a deep breath, she pricked her finger again and began to write, but still no gold would come out. Finally, on the last page, when Christine had given up all hope and was merely trying to tell well the great story she had remembered, the gold emerged anew.

As Christine continued to work at writing down the great stories, she began to see more gold emerge. It was never a large flow, only wisps and threads, but it was enough. She was as interested in the stories as the gold, and so she used the gold to add a beautiful accent to something. She was fond of weaving the gold threads into bracelets and sashes, wrapping them around girls’ braids, and giving them to old women who had been kind to her. No one knew where she found these things and she never thought to tell them.

“I never wrote down the stories to hoard them,” said Christine. “Why should I hoard the gold too?”

A few years later, when Christine was of courtly age, there was a great flood in the land. The great wall of the city had broken amid heavy rains, and the town and surrounding areas were washed out. The town had lost everything. Christine had been carried far downstream, far from her family, and left with only the clothes on her back. There was no telling where she was or how she could ever return.

Christine sat on the river’s edge, wringing out the water from her hair and clothes, her tears mixing with the brackish water. She tried to remember her town, her family, and the storytellers, knowing that it would be some time before she would see them again or even be able to write them down. After taking these things into herself, she arose and followed the road to the nearest town.

After a six mile journey, she arrived at the town of Oglethorpe and began going door to door looking for work. She inquired of all kinds of work—housekeepers, cooks, store help, anywhere that might have need, but she found nothing all day. Dejected, she trudged to the local bar to see if there might be anyone there who knew of work that was needed, and from the gloomy side of the room, an ogre’s yellow paw waved her over.

“What’re ya good for?” he grumbled towards her without staring up from his beer. “The only way you’re getting a job here in that condition is to sell something precious. Now what kind of precious thing can you do that others can’t?”

“I can make words turn to gold,” said Christine, the words escaping like breath.

“Words into gold?” said the ogre, suddenly leaning over his belly towards her. “How’s it work? How much do you get from it?”

Christine tried to tell the ogre that she wasn’t entirely sure how it worked and that it had never been a lot of gold, but he heard what he wanted.

“I’ll pay you half a dragoon for each page of gold you write. Be at Brian’s Pawn Shop at first light.”

The next day, Christine showed up to work at Brian’s shop and was handed a stack of yellowed paper and a pen.

“Write down all the words I say today,” said Brian. “I expect at least one handful of gold by the end of the day.”

Christine listened from her table in the office, writing down all that he said. Page after page filled up with foolish sayings, idle threats, general gruffness, and nothing worth the paper it was being recorded on. Christine found it strange to write for money and not for pleasure, hoping that the gold would flow from even the poor words she was recording. By the end of the day, she had filled up the entire set of pages and had a small pile of gold.

“What’s this?” said Brian. “All this paper wasted and only this gold to show for it? You’re lucky I’m a kind ogre. The other store owners would’ve thrown you out for this. You’ll have to do better tomorrow.”

The next morning, Christine collected a new stack of papers that was provided to her, tried to ignore the cramps in her hand, and tried to focus on the writing. After the morning rush, the store got quiet in the afternoon with no one coming in to talk. Christine decided to write down something about her homeland, and as she considered her words, the gold flowed from her pen as fast as the ink hit the paper. She had got almost half a page in before Brian’s bellowing stopped her reverie. Carefully she folded up the paper and tucked it behind her belt.

As Christine continued to write for Brian, she began to create more gold from his words. It didn’t flow at the same rate as it did in the precious few moments when she was recording the old storytellers’ stories and it was a little duller in color but it was still gold and would satisfy Brian. She could write for Brian during the day and write for herself in the evenings. The gold from the daytime went to Brian and paid her keep while the gold from her own writings she was able to use to slowly rebuild her life. She bought a second dress and a bag to hold her writings and few other belongings.

Brian began enlisting Christine’s help in the shop from time to time, even though he still required her to create an increasing amount of gold. But he recognized that his customers might enjoy seeing a beautiful maiden and used her to help his business.

And even though Christine’s laugh did not escape her as easily as before (as so happens when one works for an ogre), it had not lost its effect. The customers were charmed by Christine and vastly preferred her to Brian.

“Whatever is she doing working for an ogre like that?” they murmured upon leaving. “There must be something we can do.” But they dared not say anything around Brian.

A man of action named Timothy came to the shop one day when Brian was out. He quickly surmised the situation and suggested Christine should leave, but she declined.

“I am homeless, and I have no family and no prospects. This is not much, but it is a bed and a purpose. If I leave here, I lose again what I have worked hard to hold onto.”

That night, Timothy picked up his pen and began to write his own words.

Brian, you are a foul belch of a creature and a scourge upon this land. Your pattern of exploitation knows no bounds. You gorge yourself upon the labors of those you ‘employ’ and upon the food and wealth that they provide for you. If you will not release these poor creatures from their servitude to you, then you should at least pay them a living wage. For if you do not, they will despise and resent you more and more until the day comes when everyone shall turn their back utterly upon you. I am writing this for your own good—heed my words and prosper others. Timothy

Brian could not read, so he ordered Christine to read the letter. Her eyes grew wide as she saw its contents. She tried to soothe the wording, but Brian demanded she read it in its entirety. As she read the note, Christine got paler and paler and Brian got angrier and angrier. He exploded before she could finish the last sentence.

“Who does he think he is to tell me what to do? I built this business from nothing, which is as much as he knows. If I ever see him again, I’ll show him what all his so-called wise words actually profit him. And as for you,” turning to a very pale Christine, “since you were the one who no doubt encouraged this fool, then you’ll have to create even more gold, and I won’t say a word to you. Why should you be allowed to use my precious words? You’re the one making the gold; come up with your own words and stop cheating on me.”

Christine found it hard to write in the store. She could still write the conversations she heard, but that was not enough for the gold Brian required. She learned to write trifles and foolish things, just enough to create the gold needed to assuage Brian, but her heart was not in it. Although Timothy encouraged her to develop her gift on the side to earn more, Christine could not bring herself to do so. She was not interested in riches, and writing enough to satisfy Brian was a hard day’s labor. Though she still longed to record the old stories, she had no energy to do so, and the stories felt slighter and less vivid than they had before.

As Christine’s fortunes were dwindling, Brian’s were increasing. He used Christine’s gold to buy the adjoining store and begin employing the farriers. Then he melted down the next bit of her gold to make gold bars, which he could sell at great profit. As his fortunes grew, so did his pride and his belly. He had not thought much of Christine at any point, but he forgot all about her except for the gold she created.

Brian eventually moved Christine to the back of the farrier’s shop near the furnace, consolidating all his production in one store. There was a small window up too high in the shop to look out of, and the light from that was all the connection she had to the world. Brian would stop by each day for his gold, complaining that it wasn’t enough each time. It would be three months before Christine saw another soul in the store.

One day there was a visitor to the rear of the shop. The blacksmith was there to fix the furnace. Christine paid him no attention, absently lost in writing, but he noticed her immediately. “A canary in a coal mine,” he thought. He waited for Brian to leave before he approached her and asked what she was doing.

“Just writing,” she replied.

“Lady, I can’t read or write. It’s not ‘just’ writing to me. I wish I had that skill.”

Christine smiled at him. “Oh, anyone can do it. I just have to think a bit and eventually the gold starts flowing.”

The blacksmith’s eyebrows raised a foot and didn’t recede until she had finished telling him her gift. Seeing him, she ended with a laugh, “If I knew you’d have that reaction, I would’ve written something for you.”

When the blacksmith heard that laugh, he was charmed as every man had been before. But he was old enough to know the difference between the fleeting and the sublime, and to know the proper posture towards each. A twinkle entered his eye and a smile played at the edge of his mouth, and suddenly time stilled, drawing him into a memory, only beatified and savored. He stood there for minutes reliving it but found as it ended that only a few seconds had passed and he was still standing there in front of the laughing maiden with the golden words. And suddenly he felt older than he had ever been before in his mind but more hale and hearty than even the greatest warrior king had been in his youth.

“Miss,” he spoke, “your gift is not the writing. It is a gift to be sure, but it is not the gift you have been given. Your gift is your ability to bring beauty into the world. The words are the skills by which you do it, and the gold is the byproduct of it. But they are not the true gift. You have something far more precious.”

Christine treasured those words the rest of the evening, pondering them until their truth lodged in her heart. She did not feel it right to write them down, but instead wrote a sonnet, and every line turned to solid gold.

In three weeks, the blacksmith returned with the new part for the furnace. But he also had a companion with him, who was a very skilled artisan who had been asked to make a bowl for the queen. The blacksmith had told him of Christine’s gift, saying that it was the finest gold he had seen in the land, and encouraging him to work with her. He disguised the artisan as his apprentice to smuggle him past Brian and speak to Christine in private.

The artisan’s design was to be painted on porcelain, then have its edges carefully etched and inlaid with gold before being polished to make a beautiful shine. Christine’s ability to write thin lines of gold would be perfect for the dish and highlight the beautiful design.

“The bowl has to be ready for the solstice feast, and that is in one week’s time,” he said. “I can collect the gold, but we have been unable to hammer it out into the fine strands needed for so beautiful and intricate a design. Can you help us?”

Christine was taken aback but flattered, and the artisan’s design was unlike anything she had ever seen before. She picked up her pen and began to write, and the words seemingly escaped from her pen, the gold flowing finely and freely from it. The artisan and the blacksmith stood there watching this, in awe of what they saw.

Christine had finished three pages worth of gold before she looked up and asked how much was needed. The artisan was relieved, for she had created what he needed twice over, and he was now beginning to think of how he could use it.

“Could you put a secret flower in a place no one would think to look for it?” asked Christine. “The bowl is so beautiful that people will be studying it for a long time, and it would be something that they discover only after looking at it for a long time.”

The artisan clasped his hands in delight. Christine had saved the day with her gold and with her design.

“I promise you,” he said, “that this will be in the design, and you will see it with your own eyes, and that I will never forget what you have done today.”

Five days later there was an official courier to the shop. The artisan was to present the bowl to the queen and had requested Christine to share the honor with him.

“But what shall I do?” said Christine. “For I have only a tattered old dress and a plain white one. And I do not know what I shall say to the queen.”

The blacksmith overheard this and comforted her. “Write out what you would say to the queen, and take as much time as you need to prepare yourself for it. And when you are satisfied, then set it aside and give me the gold from it. My wife is a seamstress of great skill, and she will be able to prepare something for you with it.”

Christine composed herself and wrote out the scene at court, imagining the queen, surrounded by the handmaidens and courtiers, with the artisan standing alongside her. It took some time and pages, but she finally had a scene she felt comfortable in. The blacksmith took the gold and the white dress and went home to his wife.

He returned early two days later with her dress. The old white dress was now interwoven and contoured with gold, and it had a beautiful golden shawl and belt to go with it. When it was put together, Christine’s head looked like it was floating above a golden cloud, with her black tresses rippling down over it.

She turned to see the arriving artisan stop in his tracks.

“You too?” laughed Christine, and suddenly the artisan’s world became a kaleidoscope of beauty, his old designs woven into new designs that seemed to sprout from every curve and surface around him.

The blacksmith smiled and waited a moment before breaking him from his reverie and sending them on their way. Christine got into the carriage with the artisan and away they went to the castle. She could not help but take in all the sights and sounds of the town, then the countryside, then the royal mountain until they finally reached the castle at the top of the hill.

The artisan was announced and ushered in, Christine following a step behind him on his right. The queen sat on the throne at the end of a large circular room, with her handmaidens on her right and her courtiers on her left. The artisan subtly pulled Christine’s elbow, drawing her even with him before the queen.

“Your royal majesty, you have blessed our land with wise and fair rule for many years. Today, we offer a small token of thanks in return to you. The solstice feast occurs at the height of the summer, when day is longest and illumines the glory of the land. This bowl we present to you was fashioned to reflect that glory and to delight you, your majesty. The flowers on it reflect your beauty. The gold on it reflects your glory. And the porcelain is clear to reflect your fairness. Your majesty, please accept this gift from us, your humble servants.”

The bowl was carried in as the artisan and Christine stepped aside and bowed. An astonished murmur came from the crowd as they gazed on the bowl. The courtiers marveled at the handiwork and the handmaidens marveled at the beautiful flowers and gold edging.

Then the prince spoke, and all eyes turned upon him. “Maester Velsignet, you have outdone yourself. And you have left a mystery for us to discover as well. Pray tell who is your magnificent apprentice?”

The artisan reached out for Christine’s hand and stepped forward together, presenting her to the prince.

“I am Christine, your highness. Maester Velsignet asked me to assist him on this project and I was happy to do so.”

“Then you are not his apprentice?” said the astonished prince. “Such fine craft must not be your first project?”

“I hate to disappoint you, your highness, but I cannot take credit for the workmanship,” said Christine. “Maester Velsignet had the design already and I helped him with the gold.”

“But the detail which you so astutely noticed,” said the artisan, “that was her design and a result of her gifts.”

The queen relieved Christine of the spotlight, “It is an exquisite gift, and I will think of you both when I look upon it. This bowl will be the centerpiece of the solstice feast, and it will be prominently displayed when it is not in use. I thank you both for the beauty you have brought to us today.”

Christine and the artisan were smiling the whole walk back to their carriage, with many people coming up to congratulate them or voice their appreciation. But as they crossed the ballroom, the prince reappeared from a side door. He walked straight to Christine, seemingly oblivious to the artisan, and pondered her for a second before speaking.

“Miss Christine, I must ask you if you will join me. I have long been looking for someone like you who can help bring beauty to these lands. I perceive that you have gifts, and I wish to join those gifts with my own to do great things for our people. Would you do me the honor?”

A flash of time passed before Christine’s eyes in a moment, and she suddenly saw a glimpse from a tale from her homeland, of princesses and knights and maidens. And her face, which had been so carefully composed, softened into a twilight glow as a sweet but melancholy smile came over her.

“Your highness, I am deeply flattered that you would not just notice me but offer me such a gift. But I know the gift I have already been entrusted with, and I know I need to protect it from those who would exploit it. I made a foolish decision with someone I had just met. I cannot make another decision with someone I have just met, no matter how much I value your kindness.”

Christine and the artisan said nothing on the ride home, each pondering what had just transpired. When they arrived at the farrier’s, it was night. Christine could not sleep that night, pondering all that she had seen and felt. She found herself planning out a story, playing out scenes and restarting them, trying to find the right ending for herself.

Seven days later, there was again a visitor to the shop. The royal carriage stopped in front of the store and from it emerged the prince. He searched the store and then the farrier’s until he found Christine sitting at her desk. She rose hurriedly, brushing the dust from her old dress and curtseying before him.

“Lady Christine—I will not lie: I am here to ask you to reconsider. But I am also here to offer you a gift as a token of my intent.”

A dwarf strode forward bearing a pillow with a drape. Without taking his eyes off Christine, the prince pulled away the drape, revealing an oaken pen inlaid with jewels and a golden book full of golden pages.

“This pen was knit together by the great magicians of my grandfather’s court. It has the ability to create into life the things written with it. My grandfather the king ordered that it not be used until was found someone who had both the skill of how to use it and the wisdom of what to create with it. And this golden book has unending pages. It can hold all the stories that are written in it, and it also holds the magic of the stories that plays upon the hearts of all who hear the great stories.

“I do not give these gifts to anyone, but to someone I could entrust to use them well. And I would entrust more than that to you, if you are willing.”

Christine could scarcely believe her eyes. Nor could she control her hands, which grasped the prince’s as she nodded while fighting back tears of joy. “I am willing, my prince.”

At this moment, a corpulent yellowed ogre oozed its way through the doorway. “What’s going on here?” said Brian. “What’ve you done with my gold? I can take that book to cover that day last week you missed.”

Christine turned to face Brian, her tears giving way to resolution. “Brian, I am leaving. All these years you took my best and consumed it. You never cared for me nor for my stories nor for my gift but only for your income and your belly. I shall write you your gold tonight, so much that you shall not even be able to move in it. Goodbye.”

“My dear Christine!” exclaimed the prince, drawing a laugh from Christine and the company. And as she laughed, the heart of the prince and the hearts of everyone in that room save the ogre were enlightened, and they looked forward to the wedding day.

The prince arranged for Christine to stay in a royal cottage until the wedding. She was busy with the preparations, and all the handmaidens agreed it would be the most beautiful wedding in the kingdom’s history.

Christine had grown close to the blacksmith and the seamstress throughout this time, and they warmly agreed to present her to the prince. “There is something you should see, lady.” The blacksmith opened the large chest in the corner and pulled out a broadsword.

“As you know, when a prince marries a princess, her family provides a gift to the prince at the time of the wedding. We knew you didn’t have a lot of money, but we weren’t going to present you empty-handed.”

Christine marveled at the sword. Its edge was true and its steel was strong. The handle was made from hair the color of her own, and the hilt was crowned with gold and adorned with a large ruby. The belt was made from dark, supple leather and fastened with silver links. And on the blade were written the words, “I serve at the behest of my master for the good of his beloved and his kingdom.”

The blacksmith and his wife accompanied Christine to the church, and he presented to the prince. Words were said, gold rings were exchanged, and the prince kissed Christine as the multitudes celebrated their new princess.

The queen took the golden crown of the princess and put it upon the head of Christine. She grasped the hand of her prince as they turned towards the crowds.

“Welcome to your kingdom, my dear,” whispered the prince. “What kind of stories will they tell of this day?”

“We will give them one that endures,” said Christine with a laugh.

And as her laugh echoed throughout the church, the golden sunlight shone even more brightly upon her white dress and golden crown. The crowds could not see as she moved through the church with the prince, but they felt afterwards that all the frescoes upon the wall seemed to move, all the stained glass windows seemed to shine with an inner glow, and all their hearts seemed to sing for joy in response.
