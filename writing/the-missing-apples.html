The Missing Apples

Ty Clark

The farmer had known this day was coming. He had seen apples go missing throughout the summer, but this was more than just the usual pillaging of kids. He had never been the sharpest on inventory, but even he could tell that the inventory was somehow disappearing throughout the summer and fall. It felt like he could not keep apples safely stored once they were taken into bushels. And now Bill was arriving, and he would have to have a hard conversation with an old friend.

He watched the oncoming dust storm as Bill’s Charger drove the long length of his gravel drive. Straightening and sighing, he took his hat off and wiped his brow, then headed for the barn where Bill would be arriving. He grabbed the two bushels and brought them to the edge of the door. Stick to the script and be honest, he thought. You tried to prepare him.

Bill hadn’t even shut the car door before a quizzical expression took over his face. “Where are the other two bushels?”

“There aren’t any others. This is all I got.”

“Just two bushels of apples?”

“Yeah.”

“You’re telling me there’s just two bushels of apples this year? From your farm?”

“I tried to tell you, Bill. It wasn’t a good year. Lots of weird things. Just didn’t have the usual crop. This is the best of what I got.”

“I thought you meant it’d be a little short or a little more bruised. Not two bushels total. We’d be lucky to get two quarts out of this.”

The farmer nodded. It was pretty close to script, but he’d had forty-three years to learn Bill. “And look—I can understand that. But I heard you gave some apples to Cecilia the other day.”

“I gave her half a bushel for the apple pie. That was half of what I usually give her. But I just couldn’t leave her out. You know how much the pie competition means to her.”

“What you mean is you didn’t want that woman mad at you.”

“Would you want her mad at you?”

Bill smirked quickly. “No…we all know we wouldn’t want that. God knows what that woman would do if she didn’t win the competition one year.”

The farmer nodded. “I obviously don’t need my quart this year. I’ll keep looking around to see if the apples went somewhere, but…you’re probably best going to Stolefuth’s for the rest.”

“I might send Jane out for them instead. You and I would never hear the end of it if I went there. I might never get out of there too.”

They both chuckled at that one. Thom Stolefuth’s reputation and sense of superiority was well known. Bill was doing him a favor, even if he was annoyed.

“You heading over to the Festival tonight?”

“Maybe. I’ve gotta finish watering. Then I’m gonna search the place again. Besides, it’s only the art judging. How many snowy photos of Bear Creek Bridge can one see in one’s life?”

Bill nodded. “I might go check out the corn maze. Randy said he went back to doing it himself this year and was talking it up.”

“I’ll let you know if I make it.”

“Hope to see you there, Issi.”

Bill’s car recoated everything in dust as it left, taking with it the last of the farmer’s apples. He knew there weren’t more but he felt obliged to check once again for Bill. There also wasn’t a lot he could do at this point with the farm. The drought had cut back his crops this year, leaving him less to sell and thus less income. He had an irrigator, but he was tight on money by now. The money had to last through the winter. The crops would have to deal with less, just as he would.

It was dark by the time he finished his work and his searching. He stepped out of his boots on the threshold and then remembered that he didn’t have any food left in the fridge. The store would be closed by now—Dave and Luanne were always heavily involved in the fair—and it’d be past nine o’clock by the time he’d get back from the grocery store. He dusted off his boots and then his jeans, called Bill, and began driving to the Fall Festival.

He finally reached Olpecat Park, paid for the week parking pass, and headed for the food stand. The first day of the Fair was always fairly dead. The animals were more themselves and less accustomed to crowds, but otherwise, there wasn’t the sense of life you’d get later in the week with more people. The first couple nights were mostly farmers, people in the art competition, and the older crowd. He decided to quickly walk through the art barn on the way to meet Bill and his friends. As expected, it was snowy photos of Bear Creek Bridge and black-and-white horse portraits. There were a couple pictures of farmhouses but nothing he hadn’t seen before.

He found Bill at the table near the corn maze along with Jim and Ward, two of Bill’s longtime poker night buddies. Ward had a wry sense of humor that the farmer had always appreciated. “Paul, I don’t think you’re gonna want to see this one this year—it’s a fountain. Apparently Randy felt God hadn’t tortured your psyche enough this year, so he did the maze like a fountain.”

The farmer laughed along with all of them, shaking his head. They talked for a while, listening to the Baptist church singers in the background while they talked about Festivals gone by and what they expected from this one.

He was the last to leave the table. Though he was the quietest of the four, he appreciated being around them and around the Festival in general. The solitary man felt most able to untangle his thoughts when there was some life happening around him, even if he was still.

An odd noise stirred him from his reverie. It was a chuckle, distinctly a chuckle, but it wasn’t quite a child’s voice nor a man’s. A parrot? But he’d never seen a parrot at the Fair in the sixty-odd years he’d been here, and the animals were three pavilions over in the wrong direction from the sound. He was perplexed until he turned to the left and saw a dwarf standing by the far edge of the corn maze and laughing. He was also eating an apple.

The farmer’s brow deepened instantly, and he reached for his glasses to make sure his mind wasn’t playing tricks on him. Glasses revealed that yes, there was a dwarf, he was eating an apple, he was looking at the farmer, and he was utterly unconcerned by Paul. The dwarf was wearing a green hat, brown pants, and what looked like a red and brown plaid shirt. He clearly did not belong there but did not seem to mind.

The farmer reached for his phone. He knew the dwarf would probably vanish into the corn before he could get to him, but maybe he could get a photo to look up later. But there was no dwarf on the screen. He looked up. Nothing. The dwarf must’ve gone back into the corn.

The next day passed as mostly a blur. His thoughts drifted between the apples, the dwarf, and the two friends he’d disappointed. He’d known both of them for over forty years, Cecelia for his whole life. Bill had taught his sons biology at the local high school, before they moved away. Cecelia had been a close friend of his wife’s and a longtime part of his church life. Both of them got their apples from him not just because he had the best apples around but because they wanted to give him their business. And now, for no fault of his own, he’d let them down. Or was it his fault? He turned over the item in his mind. There were always kids stealing apples from the far trees, just as he had as a teenager. He’d often joked that his apple trees were repaying all the apples he stole from Old Man McGillin over the years. Still, he couldn’t shake the feeling that the dwarf had been involved somehow. And then he shushed himself, telling himself that dwarfs aren’t real and that he just wanted to be off the hook somehow.

He called it a day earlier and left for the Festival before dark. The farms on Kinnick Mill Road were even more brown and dusty than his own. If this is how it’s going to be, maybe the warehouse wasn’t such a bad idea. There hadn’t been many changes to the landscape on Kinnick Mill Road over the years he’d been coming to the Festival, which made this one feel especially sharp. Even if it made sense for Rich’s widow, it didn’t make sense to him. He’d gotten used to the smell of chicken coops wafting from Rich’s barn. Now he’d have to go to the Festival for that.

The second night of the fair was the produce judging. He was on the committee this year, though no one would tell him how he got there. All the fruits and vegetables were a little lean this year, which was to be expected with the drought. The squashes and pumpkins were always the stars of the show and did not disappoint. Though they weren’t as big as previous years, they were still solid, with good shape and minimal blight. The committee took nearly twenty minutes to decide a winner there, while picking a winner from the tomatoes was much less challenging—only two of them were even remotely good enough for competition.

They asked him if he wanted to announce the winners on the stage, but he declined. If he stood at the edge of the stage, he had a good view all the way to the corn maze on his right. If the dwarf was there again, he’d see him clearly. Unfortunately for Paul, the action on the stage kept drawing him back. Frank Seidel was announcing the winners with the gusto of someone who had waited a long time for something he knew he should not have done and was in no way qualified for, and the crowd was reacting by laughing at and with him as they cheered.

“Now we have to talk about the best pumpkin. You all remember Cinderella? I mean, not remember her, she’s not real, but remember the story? That movie? Not the remake, I heard that was bad.” Murmurs in the crowd, half in assent. “Well tonight’s pumpkin would be worthy of her carriage. And it goes to a Prince Charming who’s…well he’s not the one we would’ve picked. But he’s been proving us wrong ever since high school when he kissed Jenny Winfield! Gene Boblinksi, get on up here! You got the award for the biggest and best pumpkin this year! Come on up here and get your…glass slipper.”

By the time they got to the tomatoes, the crowd was getting bored. The kids were long gone, and the adults had thinned to mostly be the entrants, who were checking their watches and glancing around. Paul started edging his way outwards, looking for his cue to exit and for his dwarf. Sally Forsyth had just won for pears when he noticed that the dwarf had returned. He glanced around to see if anyone else had noticed but didn’t see anyone. They were more wandering around to the other pavilions or checking their watch for when the string band was supposed to start.

Slowly he started walking towards the cornfield, trying not to arouse attention or conversation. He’d just cleared the edge of the last barn when the dwarf turned and looked at him. “The winner…of this year’s best peaches…is none other than—”

The dwarf turned and mooned him. Paul stopped and stared quizzically at him. It wasn’t unsurprising, but it wasn’t surprising either, not for a dwarf with a scruffy beard and thrift store clothes. In one motion, the dwarf turned and pulled up his pants, grinning, and jumped into the cornfield with a laugh. Paul jumped as quickly as he could and followed after him, about fifteen feet behind. It wasn’t too hard to track the dwarf, who was pulling corn cobs off and tossing them towards him as he ran. Randy’s going to be mad if he sees this, he thought. Wonder if he’s got video cameras or a drone watching this.

Paul had never grown corn himself but had hidden and searched in enough cornfields over his lifetime. To find someone, you wanted to run through the rows quietly as long as the noise level stayed constant, then move towards it when it started to go faint, looking out for bruised stalks. A dwarf was harder to spot due to his short legs but also couldn’t run as fast and made marks at an obvious height. Paul tracked him fairly easily but always tried to stay at a distance. If it was truly a dwarf, then there were probably other dwarfs nearby, and he didn’t want to run into a camp of angry dwarfs.

But before long, the noise stopped. He could still hear a little bit of the Festival in the distance, but the noise of the dwarf had vanished. He stood in place for a while, wondering if the dwarf was waiting him out, but he didn’t smell anything out of the ordinary either. The trail had gone cold. But where could the dwarf have gone off to? He looked around but it was all corn stalks. He decided to walk a little further in the direction he’d last heard sound, trying to keep his eyes low and his steps quiet. If he didn’t see any holes within a ten pace radius, he’d head back.

He’d been moving sideways when his foot hit a stone and stopped. He doubled over, breaking a fall by putting his hands on the stone just before he tumbled over. There, in the middle of the cornfield, were a small series of moss-draped rocks surrounding some water. Randy had put a well in his fountain corn maze? But then he dismissed the thought. This wasn’t in the middle of the maze but off in the thicket at the edge of the maze. It wasn’t part of it at all, but it also was built into the cornfield. The well was about eight feet around but seemed to have a path on the side of it that went underneath it. Either this had emerged out of nowhere or Old Man McGillan had planted around this.

He stood there, pondering the absurdity of the situation. He had found a secret well in the middle of a cornfield where an apple-stealing dwarf lived, and this sixty-three year old farmer was contemplating whether he should crouch down and crawl into it in search of…his thief? his pride? curiosity? In the end, vanity won out. He’d come back tomorrow in his work jeans and wading boots, not his nice jeans and white polo. There’d be nothing to judge, and the fireworks would light up the area for him to find his way.

The fireworks night was the biggest night of the fair, sometimes attracting as many people as the other three nights combined. It was always on Thursday so that it could be postponed to Friday in the event of a rainout, although that wasn’t common. However, in 1987, it rained all four nights of the fair, cancelling nearly every event. This was referred to as the “Flood Festival” and was unquestionably the worst fair anyone had ever seen. This year, there’d been questions about whether the fireworks were reasonable with the drought, but no one wanted to have a “Fireless Festival.” They were on the docket as usual.

The final two nights also started the pig scrambles, which was occurring when Paul arrived. He had enjoyed participating as a child and watching as an adult, but the chaos and noise had started to get to him. The pig scramble was four pavilions away from the stage, but the noise from it would often overpower the band playing on stage. When it started, the older folks in the audience would get up and move closer to the stage while the rest of the crowd cheered on the kids.

Paul stayed mostly near the horse competitions and tractor pull on Thursdays. This was the night with the most kids running around, and it was easier to commiserate with other farmers where the kids wouldn’t come around. All of them had hard years too. Even the happier ones had thin smiles.

When the fireworks were about to start, Paul begged out. “I might try to beat the traffic. I’ve got a long day tomorrow and don’t want to be getting back at midnight.”

The other farmers said good-bye. “You better not show up tomorrow with some of your own cider,” barbed Frank.

Paul laughed with them, but he wondered what he’d find tonight. He headed back towards the parking lots at the edge of the corn maze and paused, waiting for the first firework to be launched. A whoosh was followed by a pop and a reddish glow overhead, and he began to move.

He hadn’t seen the imp tonight, but he remembered the exact area he’d seen him before and ducked in among the stalks as two whooshes followed. He had a great memory for plants and trees that acted as his compass, but he felt almost guided to the well tonight and found it quickly. In the firework light, he could see it more clearly. The well was somewhere between a tunnel and a ditch as it buried into the earth—an especially strange feature for a flat cornfield—and the water seemed to have a faint glow at the back of it. The firework light only seemed to shimmer off the top of the water. Its deep glow was never obscured.

But the path was his main focus tonight. It was only a foot or two wide and a couple feet high, seeming to curve around the side of the water—at least as far as he could see. The water glow did not appear to extend to the path. After thirty seconds, he took a deep breath, exhaled slightly as he dropped to one knee and then the other, and began to crawl onto the path. He had to squeeze low to get under the opening, going almost prone. Unlike The Great Escape, there would be no one to pull him out. He was without a rope.

He proceeded carefully and quickly got to a place where the path ended. The glow was still visible, though, and had arguably grown stronger. He lay there for a minute, puzzled and frustrated and unsure of why he was there. The only path would be through the water. He was sure he could swim a little, but he’d never been the best swimmer even in his youth. In the end, stubbornness won out. He crawled into the water.

He noticed instantly that the water didn’t feel properly wet. He was moving too quickly through it, without proper drag, as he moved towards the light. Before long the cave took a turn upwards and he swam towards it. As he broke the surface and pulled himself onto dry land, he noticed that his clothes were not sopping wet and his hair was not dropping down his neck. Whatever it was, it wasn’t exactly water, even if it looked similar.

But what was really attracting his attention was the land he was on. He’d pulled himself into a small clearing about thirty feet wide of packed dirt that was surprisingly firm and not dusty. The tall trees around the edges looked like cedars but were much taller, maybe fifty to seventy feet high, but he could see a purplish sky above them. The glow he had seen from the water was coming from the woods opposite him.

He stood up and started to walk down the path towards the glow, glancing at the forest as he went. The cedars were all in full color (much to his jealousy) but somehow there were colorful plants growing underneath them, even where light was not getting through. Everything seemed to be still in season or at least about to turn. It didn’t have the smell of fall to him, but neither did it smell like a summer forest either.

After a short walk, he found the glow coming from a large building atop a set of wide stairs that he had almost stumbled onto. The building was visible only in silhouette: there was a glow around the building and also something coming from a doorway in it too. Taking a deep breath, he carefully moved up the stairs and heard both murmured singing and quick chattering around him. The singing seemed to be from the forest while the chattering seemed to be from inside.

At the top stair everything became clearer and more confusing yet again. It was a very large banquet hall being prepared for some kind of event, and many dwarves were scurrying around to prepare it. The hall was a smooth surface throughout. It looked like stone but felt like wood beneath his feet, and he couldn’t see a seam anywhere on the structure from the floors all the way to its vaulted ceiling. It was well lit with an indiscernible glow: there were no clear light sources, but the room itself almost seemed alit. The hall was prepared with long tables running lengthwise on the edges, a wide pathway in the middle, and steps to a stage opposite him. There were trimmings hovering—yes, hovering, he affirmed—on the walls in gentle waves of amber with blue edges, as a nymph in a similar color was gliding about guiding the preparations.

He marveled at what he was seeing from just inside the doorpost, scanning the room for his green-capped suspect. They hadn’t seemed to notice his arrival, and he was hoping to spot his suspect before his cover was blown. Was that the dwarf at the opposite edge of the hall? As he shifted forward to get a closer look, his boots squelched up their water. Instantly the room stopped and all eyes turned to him. He stood stock still, less flushed than concerned. What would this group do to an uninvited guest? More than that, what could they do to him?

The answer, at least after a couple seconds, appeared to be nothing at all. The dwarfs turned back and continued their work, muttering more noisily and glancing his way often. He understood. They were on deadline, and he had until that deadline to figure out how to avoid issue. But now the nymph was approaching him and he was suddenly ashamed. He would not have been presentable before the water and certainly not in this condition. Seemingly understanding his situation, she greeted him quickly.

“Welcome, child of dust. We are not used to visitors from your realm. What brings you here today?”

“Well…I came chasing someone….I mean, I thought I saw someone who I thought might be an answer to my problem…then I found a hidden well…” he knew he was rambling, but it was hard to know what to say, especially to this creature in front of him. She listened patiently, only tilting her head slightly and smiling as he stammered. He felt disarmed, both at peace but also uneasy at not having armor.

“How did you come across this well?” she asked calmly. “It was deeply hidden, and you are the first to enter through this particular one.”

“I—there are others?”

“Oh yes, there are many wells (I believe that is what you call them). They are doorways between your world and this one. We use them often, but there are very few people in your world who know about them, let alone pass through them.”

“Who…or what passes through them? Have you ever been in our world?”

“Yes, often. I was there very recently. But now I am here to make preparations for the Feast of the Harvest King.”

“Ah. We are having a feast of sorts in my world right now. The big feast is tomorrow but the celebration has been happening for three days now.”

The nymph smiled. “It is good that our realms celebrate together.”

“Is there something that happens when they don’t?”

“It is about what you’d expect. The bigger problem comes if the Feast of the Harvest King doesn’t happen properly. Then all the realms suffer.”

“So there are consequences if your Feast is not done right? I can see quite the team helping to prepare here, but there aren’t usually problems in my world when our feasts don’t go right.”

“There are always problems whenever anything doesn’t go right,” she said, “but it all depends on what goes wrong, what depends on it, and why the issue happened. The Feast of the Harvest King has serious consequences for all of us. If it isn’t arranged properly when he arrives, then he’ll be angry, and he stays angry for a while. It gets colder in your world when that happens. If there’s too little to drink, the dwarfs won’t protect what’s in the ground for the winter. If there’s too much to drink, they run around too much and get into mischief. If I’m not there at the right time, then it stays warmer in fall but has a longer, harsher winter.”

He had more questions but also thought he had understood the key. “Did this happen six years ago by chance?”

“Ah, yes, you remember it.”

“I do. I lost a third of my crops that winter. We had a very good harvest—I was able to sell more than I ever had—but I had to spend it all the next spring to rebuild the fields. Nothing went into the ground easily the next spring either.”

She nodded and smiled. “You are a man of the soil. What is your name?”

“I’m Paul, Paul Issador,” he said, bowing. “My friends call me ‘Issi’ but you can call me anything you want. How should I address you?”

“I am Sephon, queen of the faeries,” she stated. He started to bow instantly, but she reached out her hand. “Please do not be afraid, Paul Issi. I can see you meant no harm. What brought you here?”

He glanced wistfully at the dwarfs. “I think I might know a dwarf who’s had too much to drink already.”

“Oh?” The queen was listening intently but took care not to show it. “Can you describe the one you are thinking of?”

“Well, it’s partly a hunch. But after what you said a minute ago, I think I’m starting to understand what’s been happening in my world. I’m a farmer. I’ve lost too many apples this year and I’ve never figured out why. But two nights ago I saw a dwarf just like one here at the edge of my Festival’s corn maze eating an apple. And then last night he mocked me before running into the cornfield. I’m not positive it was him—I never saw him do it—but I wonder if it’s related.”

“You might be right,” nodded the queen. “What color was his cap?”

“It was a green cap. It didn’t fit the rest of the outfit at all, but nothing in the outfit fit the rest of the outfit.”

The queen nodded and sighed. “It is as I thought. You have seen John Barleycorn. He is one of the wilder dwarfs…we often call him an imp rather than a dwarf…but he has his uses. This year, he was in charge of gathering apples for the Feast of the Harvest King.”

“Gathering?” Paul asked, surprised.

“Yes, gathering. You are right to suspect him, and he has undoubtedly been eating some of the apples, but he has also been collecting them for us. We use him because he always finds the best apples. If he has been taking from your orchards, then I hope you will take it as a compliment, even though it has harmed your crop.”

The farmer nodded, understanding the delicacy of his new situation. “Thank you; I do take it as a compliment. But there is a problem. I also need the apples for my feast. In any other year, I wouldn’t have minded. But this year, we had such a small crop that I was well short of my number in the fall. We’re going to have our feast tomorrow and we won’t have enough cider to drink.”

The queen straightened and nodded. They stood opposite each other, reflecting on their shared problems. Finally the queen spoke carefully. “What will happen if your feast does not go well?”

“Technically nothing will go horribly wrong. Someone else will win best cider, someone else will win best pie. We’ll eat and drink together. But I know what it means to Bill and Cecilia. And I know what it means to the town for them to win. They won’t win forever, but I want it to be the right kind of defeat. If they lost to someone who didn’t deserve it, they might never come back to the Fair, and then they wouldn’t take their grandkids or teach them the family recipes. And Sylvia would be unbearable if she won, too.”

They both laughed at this remark, and the queen continued to smile at him. “You are a good man, Paul Issi. I will do something to help you, something I would not do for another who did not care as much about his people.”

Turning to the dwarfs, she called one over and told him something quietly. The dwarf’s eyes went wide and he glanced sideways at Paul, but he nodded to his queen and vanished. After a minute he returned with a beautiful golden apple, which he handed to the queen. She took it in her hand, thanking him, and turned to Paul. He instinctively reached out his hand and she gently placed it on his palm. He wondered what she felt like, but she deftly placed the apple without brushing against him.

“This is one of your apples, but it has been sanctified by our world. Plant it whole in your garden tonight, and by tomorrow you will have enough apples for your feast.”

Paul tried to keep his composure as he processed everything that was happening. “Thank you…your majesty. This is a wonderful gift. But will it cause issues for you?”

“None that I cannot deal with,” she smiled. “I am the queen of my people. You are the ruler of yours. Think of this as a gift from one regent to another.”

Paul bowed deeply in thanks and turned to leave. “I doubt our paths will cross again,” he said, “but if they ever do, please know that I will be at your service. And if there is anything your world needs that I can provide, I will be happy to help.”

“Paul Issi, you taking care of your people helps me with mine. Just make sure you leave enough apples for John Barleycorn.” And as he turned to go, she called playfully, “And I wouldn’t be so sure you won’t be seeing me again.”

He turned back slightly to take her in one last time, for memory’s sake. She stood before him a gauzy amber, almost iridescent, in a thin dress that rippled down her hips and legs. Her dark eyes matched her dark hair, and her face glowed like the moon as it smiled innocently at him. Suddenly the light of the room and the water made sense to him, and the Feast of the Harvest King seemed a wedding festival new each year.

As he strode down the steps to the water, he realized that he would need his flashlight now. There was no light on the other side that penetrated the water like the light from this realm. He would have to pierce the gloom to go home.

When he emerged from the water on the other side, he checked his pocket for the apple. It had survived the journey well. His clothes too had once again felt less wet than they should have. But the most peculiar thing was that the fireworks were still going. He checked his watch. Only ten minutes had passed. But he knew he had been on the other side for at least an hour, maybe more. Whether by magic of the queen or the water, his alibi was still intact. He found his car, crept out as the final fireworks were being launched, and headed home.

He was in a daze the whole way home. The stereo stayed off, the windows stayed up, his expression barely changed, the car largely drove itself with him just holding the wheel. When he arrived home, he dutifully planted the apple whole in a small hole he dug behind the porch, changed his clothes, and went to bed. The moon was almost full and shone through his window throughout the night, giving his room a soft glow. It made no difference. He wouldn’t have slept anyway.

He arose at dawn, threw on his slippers, and opened the back door. In front of him stood a tree, a full tree, taller and wider than his house, with apples under every leaf and branch. Its bark seemed to have a golden color but its apples were red and spotless. He stood there, lost between thinking about the miracle he had just witnessed, trying to fix every detail in his memory, and simply enjoying the glint of the new sunlight upon the tree and the way the red apples seemed to fit into the morning sky.

An escaping yawn interrupted his thinking, and he shook his head, going inside for coffee. After a cup of coffee, he picked up his phone. “Good morning, Bill. I hope you’ve got a clear afternoon. I found some apples.”

It took him three hours to collect all the apples from the tree. He had considered whether it would be better to shake the tree or pick them off one by one, and in the end he decided that it was more proper to carefully remove the apples. The single tree yielded two full bushels of perfect apples. Bill wouldn’t have to cut any bad spots out of them. They could go straight into the cider press and would produce the sweetest cider.

Detail about the drive over

He arrived at Bill’s around two o’clock. Bill came out of the barn somewhat skeptical, and he got even more puzzled when he saw the apples. “How’d you lose track of these? This is the best crop you’ve ever had.”

Paul slightly shrugged. “We better start the cider if you want to be done in time.”

They went over to Bill’s cider press, which he had picked up over twenty years ago at a flea market. Bill put the burlap bag into the basin before placing the basin on top of the plate. The screw and press were removed and the grinder was placed on top. Though Bill was still spry for his age, he often let his grandkids do the physical work, and Paul was stronger than the kids were. He moved over to the grinder and began cranking as Bill fed the apples into it. As the grinder wheels turned, the apples were shredded and dropped into the burlap bag below. Due to the size of the apples, they couldn’t fit as many into the grinder as they usually did, and it took longer to grind them up.

Within fifteen minutes, the burlap bag was full. Bill was astonished. “I’ve never seen that bag get that full on that few apples. We didn’t even get through half a bushel.”

They exchanged glances, but neither of them dared react. They were up against the clock, and they didn’t want to do anything to jinx it. Paul removed the grinder and put the screw and press on in its place while Bill grabbed the first empty gallon jug and placed it under the hole in the plate, where the pressed cider would funnel through. Paul began screwing the press down, carefully proceeding as Bill made sure the cylindrical press would fit into the burlap bag opening. As the press compacted the apples, the juice began to flow into the plate and then through the hole into the jug. They filled up a gallon and a quart with the first press, then replaced the grinder and began the cycle again. The apples never seemed to run out of juice. By the time they finished the two bushels, they had over ten gallons of cider. Bill usually got that much from four bushels of apples.

Paul raised the screw, shook the bag a little, and folded the burlap in for the final press. He had just started to compress the apples when Bill stopped him. “I don’t know how we got what we got today. I’ve never seen anything like that before and I don’t expect to again. Let’s not push these apples. They gave us enough. I’m satisfied. You take enough for your quart and that’ll be good.”

As Paul pressed his quart, Bill disappeared into a drawer and reappeared with a spectrometer. He poured a small amount into a test tube and held it up to the spectrometer. 7.5, right on the dot. The sip was unnecessary but confirmed what they already knew. Bill had some prize-winning cider on his hands.

By the time they finished labeling the gallons and cleaning out the press, it was past five o’clock. Bill pulled his cider together, called the lead judge, and headed for the fair. Paul drove home to shower and change before heading to the fair. As he collected his items and closed up his house, he stopped on the threshold as a thought came over him. He stepped back inside and went over to the pantry, looking for a mason jar. Must’ve given all the big ones away, he thought. What would your wife have said to that? But he did find a small one, and into the mason jar went half of his quart of cider. Slipping it into his jacket pocket, he left the house and began his drive over.

He knew he wouldn’t be able to wait until the end to duck into the cornfield tonight. The cider and pie judging were some of the last events of the night, and he needed to be there for those. He waited until the first cry of the pig scramble when all heads were turned and plunged into the corn then. By now, he knew his way to the well perfectly, and instead of trying the path first, he stepped into the waters.

When he reached the other side, the glow had only seemed to increase, and there was the sound of music coming from above. He carefully hurried up the stairs, and when he reached the top of the stairs, everyone there gasped. Paul had gasped at what he saw in front of him. The hall was now fully decorated, a blue carpet had been rolled down the center, and all the dwarfs were lined up on either side with centaurs behind them and gryphons above them. Each of them had also gasped as the child of dust emerged into the doorway. Even the Fairy Queen, standing on the stage, had blanched slightly. Quickly she floated over to him and spoke in a very even tone.

“Paul Issi, you should not be here.”

He nodded. “I know, your majesty. I hope I have caused no harm.”

“If you are still here when Ymir comes, then there will be some harm,” she said, glancing towards the doorway.

“I’ll be quick then,” said Paul as he reached into his jacket. “You gave me a magnificent gift yesterday, and we are about to enjoy it in my world. But my parents always taught me to be thankful and to pass on the mercies that we’re given. This is a small portion of what you gave us. I thought it was only right to return it to you. I just thought I’d leave it; I didn’t want to cause a scene.” He held out the jar to the queen.

Her eyes turned to it and then back to him as her face softened. “Thank you, Paul Issi. I know that you took this from your own supply and not from what you made for others too. You have blessed us with this, and we will bless yours as well.” As she took the jar from his hand, the cider seemed to glow.

He watched it for a second, then remembered his place, bowed to her, and began to turn down the stairs. “I will see you again on the other side, Paul Issi,” said the queen. They were singing by the time he reached the water, and he was singing as he emerged back in the cornfield. The pig scramble lasted eight minutes more…he’d been under less than a minute.

His clothes had sufficiently dried by the end of the scramble, and he checked both ways before re-appearing. He started towards the pavilion where the pie judging was happening only to find people filtering out with a murmur. He checked his wrist. Eight-thirty? They must have moved up the pie judging. He spotted the attorney Mike Keady near the entrance and asked him who won.

Keady shook his head. “She lost.”

“Lost? Cecilia lost?”

“Well, she didn’t win. She tied. You know the Caldwell girl?”

“There’s three of them, Mike.”

“She’s also the Harvest Queen this year. Goodness, man, have you even been here this year?”

Paul shuffled as Mike continued. “Rion did a pecan pie that they said was one of the best pecan pies they’ve ever had. Cecilia’s apple pie was good as always, but they couldn’t decide between them. The three judges said they unanimously agreed to call it a tie.”

Paul shook his head. “How’s Cecilia taking it?”

“Oh, she’s ok. ‘Bout as good as she could be. She and Rion swapped pie pieces and both seemed ok after that. Probably best that it was to a twenty year old and not Sylvia.”

They both followed the crowd to the cider barn. There were only four contestants this year, about half what there usually were. The drought got ‘em, thought Paul before realizing that Dave Zielinski was no longer there and Max Heintzelman had been recovering from a fall. Bill might’ve won anyway without the apples, but he was definitely going to win this time. Sure enough, he did, and every other farmer clapped listlessly like an Olympic medalist hearing the winner’s anthem.

“I just wanna say—” Bill was speaking? “I just wanna say that I’m really lucky to have a great friend in Paul Issador. I know he didn’t have a great year, but he still saved every last bit of his best for me and Cecilia. Then he came over and helped me because I got mad at him for not having enough.” The crowd laughed heartily. “You’re a good man, Paul, and I want everyone to know that this was as much you as it was me.”

Paul blushed as the crowd clapped and congratulated him. He was relieved when the loudspeakers announced the crowning of the Harvest King and Queen in the main pavilion.

As they all approached the stage, the MC motioned him over. “To crown tonight’s Harvest Queen, please welcome Paul Issador!”

Paul’s head spun. “Shouldn’t this be Bill?”

The MC kept smiling to the crowd as he handed Paul the crown. “No, he insisted on you. Besides, they’re all cheering. Can’t back away now.”

Paul glanced down at the crown as he moved around and up the steps of the stage. He’d never seen the Harvest Queen crown before and was surprised at its flimsiness. It seemed barely holding together, a thin band of amber with some fall-colored grasses and berry strands woven together across the middle. He’d seen it from afar for many years and hadn’t realized that they’d never updated it. This was the same crown from when he was growing up.

He looked up to the Queen before him, dark-haired with big dark eyes in a full face, shining like the moon. She was smiling, and noticed the crown in his hands as well. “That’s so pretty,” she said innocently as he moved behind her to place it on her head. “I love that it’s got all the fall colors. I guess I’m gonna have to be careful with it though,” she said to him.

He smiled back at her. Here was a queen who seemed unaware of what she was and only focused on the beauty and people around her. Unlike the last queen, he knew he could talk with her anytime he wanted to, but he thought better of it and simply watched everything in front of him with a faraway smile.

The tables were set up before them and all the pies and cider were placed onto them. McArthur County Fair tradition held that the Harvest King and Queen got the first tastes of the winning cider and pie, then the winning contestants, then it was open to anyone who wanted some. As they began to eat and drink, the band behind them started with a jolt, causing them all to jump. None of them had ever been close to the music before to get accustomed to it. The band continued to play as they ate and drank, and they all seemed to feel the music coming from inside them as well as around them. They felt their bodies relax, their conversations take up a musical rhythm, and a song seemed to be on the tip of their tongues.

Suddenly, the Queen pointed towards the cornfield. “Look at all the fireflies!” They turned and saw a great number of fireflies emerging all at once, forming a halo around Olpecat Park. A number of them flew into the pavilion and mingled among the fairgoers. The ring of fireflies drew their eyes to the Harvest Moon above them, amber and beautiful, drawing near to them and growing larger. Among the edges of the cornfield, a number of deer poked their heads out, each looking towards the humans and towards the grass in alternating measure. A pair of great horned owls fluttered noiselessly from the cornfield, alighting on the ridge of the pavilion. They all felt like dancing, and the Queen was the first to do so. The whole company joined in quickly, raising glasses, pairing off, and cheering joyfully.

After a few rounds, the farmer began to say his goodbyes. The party was continuing, but his time was over. He was tired after the last two days and needed his rest. He noticed the boy standing at his truck from thirty feet out. He couldn’t place him, not even when he got closer, and calmly said hello when he arrived.

The boy stepped forward. “Hi, Mr. Issador? I…I have something to confess to you.”

Paul stood still, listening. The boy was still too, though his speech was nervous. “The teacher said tonight that you didn’t have the apples you usually did, and that might be my fault. I ate a number of apples from the sides of your trees throughout the summer. I knew it was wrong, but they tasted really good and I wanted them so I kept taking them. I probably ate two a week. And tonight I heard that you didn’t have all the apples, and I know that’s probably my fault.”

“That’s ok, kid. I appreciate you telling me, but it wasn’t just you. We had what we needed.”

The kid stayed unmoved. “Thank you, but I was still wrong. I’d like to make it up to you…and I’d also like to learn from you. Can I help you next year?”

Paul raised an eyebrow. “Farming life isn’t easy.”

“I know. And I have school, so I can’t help all the time. But I can come by after school and on weekends.”

“I’ll tell you what: stop by on your spring break. We’ll be starting to prep the soil for the upcoming year.”

“Thanks, Mr. Issador. I promise I’ll be a help to you.”

He smiled at the kid. “I believe you. What’s your name?”

“August,” said the kid.

“Well, August,” said Paul, stretching out his hand, “I appreciate your offer but also your honesty. Your parents taught you well. I’ll be happy to teach you what I know.”

August returned the handshake. “Thank you Mr. Issador…crazy night tonight, huh?”

Paul smiled. “Never seen anything like it. One of those things you hope you’re there for because you probably won’t ever see something like it again.”

“I’m hoping I see another one. I’m only fourteen. Feels like something like this has to happen once in a while.”

“Probably,” said Paul, opening the cab door. “You’ll have a better chance of seeing it than I will if it happens.” He got inside and shut the door then stopped and turned to the kid. “You know, I wouldn’t be surprised things like this happen all the time and we just don’t see it. We’re just too focused on what’s in front of us and miss the details. I know every inch of that Park and I’ve never seen the deer in the cornfield. I probably should’ve known they were there.”

“And the fireflies looked like a drone show. I’ve never seen so many lights in the sky.”

“Yeah, that was special. I wonder what we’ll get next year.”

He turned the car on as August stepped back, and they waved goodbye as he pulled away. It was starting to get cool, but he kept the windows down on the way home. He partly hoped a firefly might fly into his cab and partly just felt like he wanted to breathe in the night air around him.

When he got back to the house, he removed his shoes, his coat, and his clothes before laying in his bed. Something didn’t feel right, so he opened both the window and the bedroom door. The harvest moon seemed as big as the window, sweetly casting its pale warm glow at him as he drifted off to sleep.

When he awoke the next morning, the tree was gone, but some half-eaten apples were at the edge of his orchards. The deer had been back. He plucked a few apples from the trees and tossed them on the ground. They would be a peace offering to the deer in exchange for his field. He had a lot of work to do to prepare for winter, and there was no time to spend on deer problems.
