Short Stories

Dreamless Sleep

Ty Clark

Dante Ellison could not dream. He did not know why nor how it had started, but he could not remember any of his dreams. There were a couple fragments he remembered and the one time when he awoke when about to run off a cliff, but that was all he had to show for the past two decades of dreaming. Even the cliff dream was odd: he had felt himself about to run off the cliff, woken up, and been not especially concerned, like he was aware of his dream occurring. The rest of the nights were darkness. He would toss and turn for a bit, then wake up the next morning to birds chirping. Wherever his mind had been overnight, it had not documented its travels.

He hadn't put too much thought into it. After all, not remembering your dreams wasn't going to affect your waking life or cause you to lose a paycheck. But it always knawed at him. Why did his friend always come into work talking about his extremely vivid dreams? Was it a problem for him not to dream? He had never felt a lack partly because so many of his friends shared theirs with him. What he lacked on his own he received from others, and with that he was content. Mostly.

But like any repressed feeling, curiosity would get the better of him from time to time. He listened to stories about dreams, especially the Dune mythos. He enjoyed thinking about the symbols in dreams and had occasionally tried to research what the few dreams he remembered might have been showing. He had tried to dream on command, adjusting his bedtime or falling asleep routine to see if that would work. It didn't, but thus began a series of experiments. First he tried sleeping in a different room of the house. Next was the doctor. After that was drinking heavily. Then he lit a candle. He tried sleeping with someone in the same bed. That one didn't sit well with him, so he confessed it to his priest one day.

"You are confessing what you did in order to dream? And it did not happen?" Father Joseph paused to absorb the humor of the situation. "Normally I receive confessions for impure dreams or questions about dark dreams. I cannot think of someone who has confessed about not dreaming. Why does it matter to you that you cannot dream?"

Dante's eyes lowered in sad reflection. "Does God not speak to us today? We have his word and his spirit; why are they still for me? Why do others hear his voice but I do not?"

"Even the prophet Samuel had to call for God. 'Speak, Lord, for your servant listens.'"

"But even Samuel believed what he heard was a man's voice. What if I cannot hear Him? What if I have stopped up my ears and cannot remove the wax?"

"Do you not believe God can work even through that? The blind man whom Jesus healed had scales fall from his eyes. He was not healed of his own will. God speaks in many ways to many senses. Even if you do not hear him right now, you may still be experiencing him."

"Perhaps. I just wish he was clearer."

The priest smiled. "So do we all."

They both sat in the darkness. Dante eventually broke the silence with a question. "Do you think I could sleep in the sanctuary tonight?"

"I don't see why not," said Father Joseph. "It is not the easiest place to sleep, but you are welcome in God's house."

Father Joseph had not lied. The pews were wooden, stiff and creaky. The seats were not wide enough for a full person, so his arm hung off the edge of the seat unless he wedged himself into the back corner on his side. And there was a distinct lack of soft surfaces to be his pillow. He slept on a Bible, neck straining, and thought of Jacob lying with his head on a rock, beholding the angels traveling up and down from heaven. He thought of Jacob wrestling with God throughout night only to lose in the morning. He thought of Jacob serving Laban for seven years only to receive what he had not wanted. He tried to pray, for it was too uncomfortable to relax, but prayer did not help him fall asleep. His prayers began to be more frantic, asking God why he could not fall asleep when he was trying to be nearer to him and his hand. Finally, after running out of things to pray for a second time over and exhausted, he fell asleep on the pew.

He slept through the birds and awoke to Father Joseph kneeling beside him to check on him. Painfully he arose. "I hurt, but I am at peace," he said. "I don't feel uncomfortable even though I am."

Father Joseph lifted his hand and recited the blessing. "May the peace of God that passes all understanding guard your heart and mind in Christ Jesus."

"Amen, so let it be."

The peace Dante felt lasted about a day. As his body ached less, his heart felt less peaceful. He found himself in situations where he did not know what to do nor what counsel to give others who came to him. He could see paths that made sense, but he did not feel peaceful recommending it.

He made a vow to himself and to God to keep Lent for the Easter season. For some reason that felt right to him. He had never done it before, but the idea of committing yourself to God felt like it might focus him. He decided to give up alcohol for the season. It would be challenging, but he could make his way around the social obligations where any questions might emerge. He also began to pray nightly before bed, briefly, trying to get into a ritual and to become closer to God. He was able to keep that practice with few lapses throughout the month, and he abstained for the whole season. Still he did not dream.

At the Good Friday service, he chose a cup from the grape juice ring instead of the wine as he would usually choose. As he left that day, he talked with Father Joseph for the first time since their conversation. "I saw you chose the grape juice and not the wine," said the priest. "What are you trying to do? Has it brought you closer to God?"

"I feel more aware of my own shortcomings and I am more prayerful. But I feel nothing else," said Dante sadly.

Father Joseph pressed gently. "What do you pray for?"

"For whatever comes to my mind. For my own sins, for others in this church, for the church itself. For my friends. For my cares and concerns. For the life of the world."

He continued, "I know I should be reflecting more on Christ than on this world, but it is hard."

"Christ commands us to come as little children," said Father Joseph. "Have you seen a child be reflective? Do you think you are not that?"

"So you are saying I should sleep in the nursery," quipped Dante, not sure where this conversation was going.

"How do children learn? By repeating the words and actions so they take root. But they follow the words and actions of another. It is by fixing their eyes on their father and talking with thim as they walk in his ways."

"I am trying, but it is hard when I do not hear my Father's voice."

"Even the twelve who heard their master's voice fell asleep. It befalls us all. Continue to pray. Bring your requests to your Father. Be at his table. Abide with him."

He slept in the sanctuary again that night, this time in view of the Remembrance Table. It was less chilly this time and he was more prepared. He awoke to light streaming in through the east windows and a cardinal and wren fighting it out for whose song would be heard most clearly.

It was the final day of Lent. He went about his business, cleaning his home and preparing food for Easter. As he was baking his bread, he received a text from Richard, his old friend. Richard was back in the area for Easter dinner at his parents' house nearby, and he invited Dante to catch up that night. They returned to their favorite tavern, as they had done for many years, but Dante ordered soda instead.

"You doing a sober spring?" asked Richard, finishing his third pint.

"No, just something for Lent," said Dante. "Trying something different this year."

"That's awesome," said Richard. "I love that kind of personal transformation stuff. I've wondered what that would be like, to just give up alcohol altogether for a while. I wonder if it'd be more like a cleanse or a long term diet. I bought a Faraday cage and have been limiting my screen time to 18 minutes a day. Revolutionary. Cannot overstate the benefits."

"I'm not sure that's quite the same," said Dante. "I'm doing it to try to draw closer to God. You're focusing on personal transformation."

"Drawing closer to God is a personal transformation. If you're trying to become a better person by adding these routines to your life, then how is that any different than me? Same goal, just different methods."

"But you're trying to get something out of it," said Dante. "You're doing it because you think it will help you get what you want more effectively."

Richard shook his head. "It's still the same root. Gamifying things is what helps them stick. Admit it: you're gonna reach for the best scotch you can find at the end of this, and it's gonna taste amazing because you're been trying to train yourself to not want this thing. It's not. It'll taste good, but not amazing. The craving never stops. You just have to keep finding ways to deal with it." He paused. "But if you can't be honest with yourself that you want to be better, then you're just gonna keep trying, failing, and falling behind."

Dante stared into his soda, trying to find the right words. "But it's why we want to be better. I'm doing this because I know it's good for me to do that."

"And I'm giving up my phone because it's a poison and it's good for me to not have that."

"So what is the thing you're trying to become?"

"Just better as much as I can be. Healthier, more focused, more productive, more present."

"You will be chasing that vision for a long time."

"As will you."

"Who will you be doing it for? Or with?"

Richard shrugged. "A better me can do even more good for the world. I have to master myself before I can help others. Doesn't matter who comes with me."

The words echoed in Dante's head after they parted that night. He drove towards home unsettled, then turned and went to the church. It was late, but not unreasonably so, and he knocked on the parsonage door. A few knocks brought Father Joseph to the door, puzzled.

"You are right," said Dante. "I have been so concerned that I cannot hear God's voice myself, but now I realize it was just for my own desire."

"What made you realize this?"

"I was with my friend tonight. He said that my observing Lent alone was the same as his hustle self-improvement mindset. We're both doing it for ourselves, apart from others. I'd like to believe I'm doing it for other reasons, but I don't think that's true. You were trying to get me to see that before, but I couldn't. Thank you for ministering to me."

"So what did you do for your brother?" Father Joseph's expression did not change much.

Dante stared back in mild shock.

"He spoke words of truth to you and you left him to speak to me? You could see me tomorrow. You could be speaking to him, sharing with him, listening to him. If you realized you both were lost, then why did you leave him lost and alone?"

Dante immediately left the step and called Richard, but there was no answer. He texted him but did not hear much. He drove back to the tavern, but Richard was not there. He walked around the town, looking for Richard, but he did not see him even as the clock passed midnight. Finally, at 2:00 am, he returned home and fell asleep in his own bed.

He awoke the next morning and checked his phone. Seeing no reply from Richard, he pulled on his clothes and went back out to find him. But there was a second car in his driveway blocking him in, and sleeping in that car was his friend.

Dante stared for a second, then went back inside and began to make breakfast. As he was finishing the eggs, the door opened and Richard shuffled in.

"Bet you didn't expect to find me here," said Richard, sipping the coffee.

"I did not. I wasn't sure you even knew where my house was."

"I didn't. Just got really tired when trying to drive home and found a driveway where I could catch some sleep. I didn't expect it to be your house."

"Strange providences," nodded Dante, serving the hot cross buns. "I am glad you are here though. I thought a lot about what you said afterwards. There was truth there, given from an old friend. I was wrong to have dismissed it so quickly."

"Hey, I'm glad something I said was helpful. It's not often that I get to play that role for you."

They ate relaxedly, enjoying the time with each other. Richard kept an eye on the clock and eventually stood up to leave. "I know you've got church this morning, and I don't want to make you late."

"You're welcome to come. There will be wine, and there will be fellowship."

Richard smiled. "I have other commitments today, but the next time I am in town, I'll make time."

The church was predictably swarming with people, and the nursery was overwhelmed. Since he had no commitments that service, he offered to help in the nursery, but the nursery keeper declined. He sat in the main sanctuary, not far from the pew where he had slept. For some reason, he found it oddly hard to rejoice that day. He was at peace, he knew it was the day of rejoicing, he had repaired his relation with his friend, he wasn't all that tired, but still he found himself willing a smile into existence. The Supper felt more suitable to his mood, and he took the wine with gratitude. He had forgotten the enlightening of the wine over his fast, and it landed more deeply than usual for him.

After the service, he celebrated an Easter supper with his extended family. There was laughter and the joy of children running around, and there was glorious amounts of food. The food rested well in his belly, and he rested well on the couch.

He awoke in a forest. A hunter was moving slowly through the damp fogginess, trying not to disturb the peacefulness as he searched. There was a quick flash of color from his left, and he spun in time to catch a glimpse of a beautiful red fox lilting away. He had no time to think. It was just at the edge of his range, and he lifted his gun only for it to jam.

The hunter began to chase the fox on foot, but the fox stayed perfectly out of range at each turn. They chased for nearly an hour, and eventually the hunter pounded a log in anger.

The fox turned around. "Why are you chasing me? You are not supposed to catch me now."

The hunter stared quizzically. "What am I supposed to do?"

"You are to discover the rare birds and bring them back to the people."

"Which birds?"

"I cannot tell you that, for I am chasing the birds myself."

The hunter raised an eyebrow.

"We both know I have the skill to catch them. You will have to beat me to them."

With that, the fox dropped its shoulders and began moving furtively along the ground. The hunter looked to see where the fox was staring, then began moving sideways to intersect the fox's path. As the fox crept quicker, so did he. He was gaining on the fox, but not quickly enough. The birds were just ahead, and the fox would there first. He stopped, cleared his weapon, and fired it in the air. The birds scattered in all directions.

"You fool," snapped the fox. "Now I will go hungry and you have lost your reward."

"I thought it best to spare its life," said the hunter casually.

"And in six months the forest will be overrun with them, for they compete with other birds for food."

"You're missing one thing," said the hunter.

"And that is?"

"I didn't come for the birds."

The fox turned and saw the gun pointed at him. It was a double-barrel shotgun, and the hunter had only fired one shot. Only the hunter knew how many shots were left in the chamber.

"I will offer you a deal," said the hunter. "Once a year, I will come back here and chase you to a new bird. Whoever gets to the bird first keeps the bird. At the end of your days, I will return for you."

"I accept your terms," said the fox. And dashing quickly between the hunter's legs, he yelled "tag, you're it," nipping him on his right leg.

Dante yelped as he sat bolt upright. He swung his leg over to the ground, but it was in pain from sleeping on it so long. He sat there, reflecting as the limb came back to life, then walked over to open the door. A blackbird perched outside quickly fluttered away to a tree. Then his phone chirped with a message from his friend.
