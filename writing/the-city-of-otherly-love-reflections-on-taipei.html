The City Of Otherly Love: Reflections on Taipei

Ty Clark

There are few things that recalibrate your sense of self-worth like spending time in a country where no one speaks your language. If not for the kindness of others who have learned your language, you are stuck and useless. You are out of place, have little to offer, and can do nothing except hope for kindness. You are at the mercy of others.

Over time, it is possible to catch repeated words and understand their meaning. In Taiwan, the word you hear most often is xie xie—thank you. It is everywhere, embedded at the end of every conversation, and frequently in the air surrounding it. Someone delivers a package and both people say xie xie. You take an Uber and the driver says xie xie. A conversation ends less often with “goodbye” than with a mutual xie xie. The word slides out as a breath, she-zheh, quickly and often.

Taiwan is known for its hospitality in multiple, often incredible ways. Corner stores have far more than the usual items and are known for accommodating requests for goods and services far outside their scope. The receptionists and managers are always smiling and willing to accommodate. On the last day, my friend and I left our lunch table before ordering because we found a promising restaurant. We returned afterwards looking for dessert and got the same waitress, who cheerfully sat us at a better table than we had been at before. When we all had to join a work call at 3am, our work host not only allowed us to use the office (and its stable internet connection for it) but picked us up in his personal vehicle to drive us over and join the call with us. “If it ends early,” he said, “I’ll have to take you all to some of the best breakfast spots.” (Alas, it did not.) The night before, he had taken us out to dinner for the third straight night, all at exceptional venues, and had brought in a couple of his limited edition whiskys for us to try.

After a while, the remarkable becomes unremarkable and you start to notice new details, which is that no one in the city seems hurried or inconvenienced. They work diligently and accommodate US hours far more than we do, but they never seem flustered by it. The traffic is chaotic and aggressive but still orderly, in a way. The pedestrians all seem to walk at the same ambling speed of the animated figure in the crosswalk sign. Despite the fullness of life in downtown Taipei, there is a calm to it distinct from other big US cities. Even laid back Los Angeles still has an edge and an undercurrent of sadness and striving.

But Taipei is busy. The home of the world’s semiconductor industry, Taipei is home to many, many operational hubs for global companies, many of which are household names: Foxconn, Benq, Amazon. The buildings downtown are a mixture of apartment buildings and gleaming skyscrapers that could be in any city. There are a lot of them, and driving from the Marriott to the airport reveals that scale differently than being on the ground. There is almost a sameness to them as they stretch across the middle distance. In front is the park system, behind the mountains. In between are buildings and cranes building more buildings.

My lunchtime friend had last been to Taipei twenty years earlier. He noticed the changes from his last trip, and so had others. The city had exploded as the consumer electronics industry boomed. But the geography of the city presents unique challenges. Taipei is at the bottom of a large bowl, which means that any typhoon or strong rain will drain to the river running through the city. Taipei built its parks on the inland surrounding the river, creating large open space that water can overflow and rain into. The parks are connected by a long trail running throughout the city, nearly fifty miles. As for the challenge of earthquakes, Taipei 101, the once-tallest building in the world, solved that problem by suspending a giant metal ball at the top of the tower. When the ground quakes, the ball moves via gravity, transferring the energy away from the building structure.

The city has grown through shrewd planning and ingenious design. Good design comes from constraints, and Taipei’s mountains always provide a reminder of that. No matter how many skyscrapers are created, no matter how far the buildings stretch, the mountains are always visible behind them, capped by clouds that shroud and roll over the peaks. Between the mountains, heat, and rain, there is a constant sense that you are at the mercy of nature. It is not something that doesn’t affect you or can be gritted through during a couple seasons.

Our second night of dinner took us up into the mountains, giving us breathtaking views of the city, but veiled by trees. It is surprisingly easy to miss it, but Taipei has more trees organically mixed into its downtown than most major US cities I’ve been to. Our Taipei offices are two fifteen-story buildings next to each other, and there is a tree in the courtyard in between them reaching up to the top floors. Trees pop up all over the streets, hug the levee walls, and bathe the city in green. There isn’t a “Hidden Gardens of Babylon” to be seen here, but the overall environment has that feel.

And with that backdrop, why shouldn’t the city be hospitable? This small island has managed to carve out a 21st-century economy for itself, with huge prosperity, in a place where nature looms in the background. For now, business and nature seem to have found a coexistence. But living among things that were designed to adapt to nature serves as a good reminder of your own insignificance. Plan all you want, but life does not respect your plan. You must plan, but you must learn to adapt as well. Taipei has learned that lesson, and it demonstrates it in its planning but blesses others with it in its hospitality.

So what if the person’s need is inconveniencing you? For as much as your paths cross, your plans each are at the mercy of each other’s. You must adapt. If you have learned how to adapt, you must give that abundance to others. And so Taipei feels at peace in its striving and its looming tensions. The threat of Chinese invasion is ever present, just beyond those mountains and the strait, a potential natural disaster of a different kind. But what can you do? Taiwan’s connections to global companies are both its allure and its strength. It’s easy to see why China would want that for its own, but everyone knows that others would rally to support Taiwan given its importance to the global economy. If Taiwan falls, so does the consumer electronics economy. This little country holds more cards than its size gives it right to thanks to its shrewdness. It welcomes people from all over the world constantly, doing so with the same xie xie in all situations.

Perhaps that is why it has endured, managing to blend the cultures who started it into one of its own and building a global city amid some unique challenges. It may not be a place that has taken your tired, your poor, your huddled masses yearning to be free, but its ability to welcome the jet-lagged, the wealthy, the tech executives used to being seen stands as a gentle critique to those nations’ current cultures. The nation that might lose all it has built at any moment does not demand to be recognized for its work but instead uses its opportunity to bless others. Christianity may not be part of the cultural heritage of Taiwan, but it is hard not to reflect on this sacrificial, incarnational approach to life and hope that the end of Taiwan’s story has a vindication like the one in the Biblical story foundational to the West, one where any wrongs are righted, where the faithful are rewarded, where good deeds done to the least of us are counted as done to the greatest, and where the threat of the sea is no more. That will likely not be in any of our lifetimes. But until then, we receive life as the gift it is and return it in grateful, gracious service to others. Xie xie, Taiwan.
