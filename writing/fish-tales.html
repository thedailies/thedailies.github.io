Short Stories

Fish Tales

Inspired by “The Fish” by Elizabeth Bishop

Ty Clark

Arbutus Jackson yanked the line and the boat engine choked for its life. He stood watching it for a second as it shuddered.

“Dang, Lester, you really musta been busy. She ain’t usually this bad. My uncle Dennie once took a boat with a bad engine off the coast and the engine wouldn’t start whe—”

“That engin was terrible,” said his companion irritably, which was his default temperament, even with his longtime fishing partner. “Uncle Dennis always took the boat out to clean it. It’s a boat. It stays in the water, it runs in the water. If you take it out, then all the tubes dry out when it’s away from the humidity.”

“I dunno Lester.” Arbutus scratched his head. “If there ain’t so much humidity on the land, then how come the meteor people are talkin bout ‘relative humidity’ all the time?”

Lester side-eyed his partner, who was continuing to confabulate. “I asked Ma bout it once and she said we didn’t like those relatives anyway. I said ‘who, the humidities? I thought we were Jacksons.’”

“You are Jacks’ns, your Ma was a fool, and I’m gett’n’ hotter standin’ here.” said Lester, undoing the rope fastening them to the dock. “You loaded all the gear?”

“I got the tackle boxes, the poles, and the cooler. But you better watch yer mouth about my Ma. She used what she had, an’ more than that, she was my Ma. She did her best to raise me the right way.”

“If she was raisin you the right way, she would’na dropped you on your head.”

Arbutus opened his mouth but was cut off. “Yes, that was only cuz you bit her, I know. You know what else are bitin? Bluefish. Let’s go.”

Lester stepped into the boat and grabbed the throttle as Arbutus continued his defense of his Ma (a subject both of them had debated over the thirty-three years they had known each other and continued to debate as they sailed down the Satilla). Once they got to the Hole, Lester killed the engine and picked up their rods. His brow furrowed as he felt something unusual on his partner’s rod.

“You got a knot in your line.”

“Oh, that’s cause this back half is dental floss. Angela got mad at me for goin out last time and musta thrown out all my extra line. I grabbed my rod this mornin and found it had maybe ten feet left. So I grabbed her dental floss and tied it to the old line and wound it back in. I heard someone one time caught a marlin with dental floss and I never forgot it. So I figured today I’m gonna catch me somethin big.”

“When’s the last time you caught a fish? Let alone a marlin?”

Arbutus nodded but remained undeterred. “it’s been a minute. But today’s gonna be the day I get my big’un.”

“Because of the dental floss? You realize that’s the thing dentists give away because it cuts yer gums so you have to go back there for surgery, right? You put that on yer line and it’s just gonna cut up all the fish. Yer gonna have bloody angry fish on your line trying to get away from you while you try clean’n’ its teeth.”

“Well maybe fish need dentists too. How you figure they always have such good teeth?”

“Maybe we’re catchin all the dumb ones,” Lester said dryly, not wanting to continue this line of imagination.

“Even the dumb’uns need dentists. I bet there’s some fish who’s always need’d a dentist and can’t get one because it’s too expensive.”

“So yer proposin to be Dentists Without Borders but for fish?”

“There’s a Dentists Without Borders? I’ve only heard of the Doctors but that woul’ be wonderful for people like me.”

“Right—people.” Arbutus shrugged. “I suppose. But fish are living things too. God created them on the…I forget what day.”

Lester stayed quiet, both because he hoped the thread would stop and because he did not want to be witnessed to.

“Anyway,” said Arbutus after a minute, “I bet you could earn good money as a fish dentist. Rich people always got those aquariums with big fish, shiny fish, all kinds. They got to care about their fish’s teeth. And they spend money on all kinds of things.”

“Nah, not on their fish,” said Lester, seeing an opportunity to pivot the conversation. “Rich people just want things to make them feel better than other people. If you wanna make money off a rich person, you gotta do somethin that makes them feel better than their friends.” He paused, letting his wisdom sink in. “That’s why I’m gonna go into bus’ness as a fish cleanin service.”

Arbutus looked up from his line. “Like…wiping them down?”

“No, no. Cleanin: slicin open the fish, taking out the spine and head and tail.”

“But don’t they do that themselves?”

Lester scoffed. “You think a rich person wants to get their hands dirty? They want someone else to do the dirty work. They want us to be their hands. Because if somethin happens, then we get cut off. They want us holdin the knife in front while they’re puttin it in our backs.”

They were silent for a minute. Lester basked in the glow of his wisdom while Arbutus struggled with it.

“Lester, I think that’s an uncharitable thing to say about someone,” he said quietly while staring at the water. “I don’t believe you can see the hate in someone that way and not look for their good too. You say all kinds of stuff about Mr. Derek but he let you have the best day of the year off to come out fishin. He can’t be that mean and that nice to you now.”

“Mr. Derek,” said Lester tersely, “only hires people he likes and can take advantage of.”

“Then how did you start workin for him? Feels like you’re not taking care of the needle in your own eye.” Arbutus’ grasp of Bible truths had always been better than his memory of their details.

“Well I’m workin on teachin ‘im a lesson from the inside,” said Lester confidently. “I’m gainin his trust, and he’s listening to me more and more. Soon I’ll be talkin to ‘im man to man. Then I’ll have his ear. You and I won’t be fishing on this boat much longer, eh, Booty Boy?”

Arbutus shrugged. “This boat ain’t bad. It’s honest. An’ it fits you and me.”

“But imagine if you could bring Ma with you. Or even that preacher’s name you’re always telling me about…”

“Reverend Tom,” Arbutus said quietly.

“Right, Reverend Tom…’im and his family. Hell, you might even be able to bring your whole church on that boat!”

“But yer my friend. We go fishin. It ain’t what my church does. They’re always doing their food and their charity work. It works out good for me. But that’s because they want to, not just because someone’s talked them into somethin.”

Lester shrugged and shook his head. “Well don’t blame me for trying to be charitable. I was just trying to offer my longest friend a chance to fish off an eighty foot yacht with all his frie—”

Arbutus’ line jumped to life, nearly jerking the rod from his hands. He caught himself, bracing his feet against the side of the boat as the rod bent like a bow. “This thing’s got some weight to it!” he said, angling the rod to relieve the tension.

The fool and the fish faught, each trying to wear each other down. Slowly Arbutus began to reel in the fish, a half turn at a time until he saw the knot in the fishing line appear.

“He’s gotta be near now! Come on over and scoop ‘em up!”

Lester grabbed the net and waited to see his friend’s catch emerge. Finally, after a ten minute battle, the fish came into view. Lester swooped in perfectly, and the two men crashed back into the boat with the fish. Once it had finished thrashing, the two men looked at the fish.

“That’s a big-ass grouper,” said Lester. “Heck of a catch.”

Arbutus said nothing as he studied the fish. He could feel the barnacles all over the fish’s brown skin. A green weed dangled from the fish’s mouth, draped over one of the five hooks attached to its jaw. “Look at that!” he marveled. “This one’s escaped five times! What kind of a life must he have had!”

“He ain’t gonna be Steve McQueenin’ out of this one,” said Lester, readying the cooler.

Arbutus was still gazing at his fish, darting between the five hooks and the fish’s yellowed eyes, when he heard a voice that wasn’t Lester’s. “I can tell you the story of each hook.”

Arbutus’ eyes bulged. Shrieking, he dropped the fish back in the boat, causing Lester to spin around. The two men staggered to their feet, peering at the fish, which was basking in the sun.

“As I said, I can tell you. But I’ll need water very soon.”

Arbutus looked to Lester, who was poleaxed by the talking? yes, talking fish in front of him. He moved to grab the cooler, which shook Lester from his reverie.

“Wait,” said Lester, as Arbutus turned to fill the cooler with water. “It’s a talkin fish.”

“Well don’t you think it deserves to be heard? I ain’t never seen a talkin fish before.”

The fish lay motionless. “B’sides…I caught it. It’s mine to be responsible for. You catch your own talkin fish, that one’s your responsib’lity.”

Arbutus grabbed the fish, being careful not to squeeze too hard, and plopped him in the cooler. The fish disappeared below the surface, its gills furiously working. The two men stared into the cooler for a while, each pondering if the fish would reappear, which in time it did, tilting so that its eyes and mouth were above the waterline while its gills remained below.

“Thank you gentlemen. It’s always a struggle.”

There was a pause as the two men once again absorbed the talking fish in front of them, then they both spoke at the same time. “Where did you learn to speak?” “I wasn’t trying to hurt you, I promise.”

“I know,” said the fish. “It’s a game that we both play, your species and mine. I’m usually better at this, but I didn’t see your line.”

“Better at what?” said Lester. “You got five hooks in your mouth.”

“Yes, but there were many more that are not. I’ve stolen bait more times than you would believe. And not all of these were my fault.”

“Mister Fish,” said Arbutus, who had lowered himself to be eye level with his captive, “I really would like to hear about those hooks, but I don’t wanna be impolite. I’m Arbutus Jackson, and this is my friend Lester Stick. We’re from Woodbine.”

“You’re telling a fish…who lives in the sea…the town on the land we’re from??”

Arbutus’ brow furrowed as he turned over his shoulder. “It’d be unhospitable not to. That’s how we was raised.” He turned back to the fish. “I’m sorry, Mister Fish. You’ll have to forgive him.”

“No offense taken,” said the fish. “My name is Howarth. I grew up south of here, near the marshes.”

“How’d you get up here?” asked Arbutus.

“My kind often migrate up the coast as we get bigger. Once we’re big enough to avoid predators, we start making our way out to the ocean. And I know: how can he say he’s avoided predators when he has all these hooks. Well, not all predators are humans, and not all humans are predators. Most of my life can be told through the story of these hooks.”

“Which one didja get first?”

“The first hook I got was this heavy line on my left. I was two and full of life, and I determined to break the strongest line anyone ever had. My brother Berley had broken a spine-width line the previous month and wouldn’t stop bragging about it. So I had to beat him, or else I wouldn’t hear the end of it.”

“This sounds like you and Jason with that deer,” said Arbutus to Lester, making him even more quiet and surly. “I’m sorry, Mr. Howarth. Yer sayin you wanted to get caught?”

“I wanted to bite a hook, and I didn’t pay much care to the hook or its setup, just to the size of the line. I wanted something big and I wanted to break it immediately. So I did. I found the first big line I laid eyes on and bit it hard. I bit so hard I yanked the rod out, and he had to grab it quickly so I could actually break the line.”

“Just how do you break a line?” said Lester from the back.

“It usually comes down to wearing out the line,” said the fish. “There’s ways to make that happen faster, but few of those exist in open water. I got lucky with mine. I ended up going past his propellor (which was fortunately not spinning) and the line cut on it. My jaw had to recover for a week, but I was swimming normally within the hour.”

Lester probed again, “How long does it take your jaw to get better?”

“Now? This one will take a month. I can still taste the blood in my mouth. It gets worse as you get older.”

“How old are you?” Arbutus asked innocently before quickly adding, “if you don’ mind me askin.”

“I am nine years old,” said Howarth. “I will be ten next spring. I am older than my father was when he was caught and two years younger than the record for my school.”

“You’ve had quite a life, Mister Howarth. All these hooks…did you get them when you was young?”

“No, but the next one I got was less than a year later. It was right after my birthday, in the spring. The water was warm, the sun was back, and I was in love with a beautiful hen.”

Arbutus was confused. “Like…a…a chicken?”

“No, silly,” said Lester, slapping him. “A hen like a female fish. His own kind. You been fishin how many years and you nev’r ‘eard that?”

“I just thought they was called fish,” said Arbutus. “I never thought that other things could be hens besides hens.”

“My hen had a beautiful tail,” said Howarth cooly. “She would look at you over one side before shaking her tail and wriggling off so quickly. You’d be chasing through the bubbles trying to see her clearly. She was playing me like you’d play a skate, and I fell for her.

“The line I snapped helped my popularity with my school, and I tried to leverage that with the hens. Talant—that was her name—and I flirted with each other for a little while. I knew she was interested in another fish, so I tried hard to please her…a little seaweed bouquet here, a little morsel there. She warmed up to me, and eventually started having me chase her.

“One day out past the marshes, we saw a line with a freshwater lure. She dared me to break it. I said this was foolish. Why would a freshwater lure be in open water? She of course called me a coward and said I didn’t love her, and so I had to prove her wrong. I bit the line…fought it for a while. Eventually I won. But when I made it back to the marshes, she was gone. I looked around for her but couldn’t find her anywhere. She had gone back to that other fish. I was the bait of jealousy she used to reel him in. They moved inland, raised some roe…at least that’s what I heard. I never saw them again.”

All sat silently, each for their own reason. Arbutus was the first to speak. “I’m sorry, Mister Howarth. No one should have to lose their first love.”

“Thank you,” said the fish. “But I learned a valuable lesson that day. I vowed only to chase hooks when there was a good reason.”

“Oh, fer cryin out loud,” said Lester from the back. “Yer gonna listen to this? To life lessons from a fish?”

Arbutus turned in surprise. “I don’ see what’s the matter with the life lessons comin from him. You never gave me life lessons from your own life.”

Lester stood up and was getting more exasperated with each sentence. “That ain’t the point, Booty Boy! He’s playin you! That fish is tryin to get out by preyin ‘pon your sympathy. You think he ain’t married? You think he ain’t got a whole fam’ly of little groupers swimmin round our boat and a byoo-tee-full whatserface back at home that he wants to get back to?”

“Well maybe he does. But is that a problem? Ain’t he got a right to want to get back to them?”

“Want ain’t got nothin to do with it, Booty Boy! That ain’t the way this world works. He bit the hook, and now he’s here. In three hours he’ll be on my table. We’re the predators; he’s ours now.”

“He’s mine,” said Arbutus indignantly. “I caught ‘im. If he’s on any table, it’s on mine. But I want to listen to ‘im talk. He ain’t done anythin to us b’sides showin us thankfulness and genrosity sharin his life story. Why’d you go straight to suspicion anyway? You caught in a trap you can’t walk out of.”

Lester rolled his eyes. “An Elvis song? Yer lecturin me with an Elvis song?”

“They called that man the King. Kings is smart.”

Lester shook his head and turned away. He snatched up his reel and seated himself again, looking down at his line. Arbutus watched him for a second, then turned back to the fish. “You said you only’d chase a hook if there’s a good reason. Was chasin mine one of those?”

The fish appeared to chuckle. “No, that was just a mistake by me. I couldn’t see your line.”

“I told you the dental floss worked!” shouted Arbutus joyfully. Lester remained unmoved.

“I’m sorry, Mister Howarth. I tried dent’l floss ’nstead of fishin line today. I ain’t caught a fish in three years, so I was all excited to catch you. It seemed my idea worked for once.”

“It did. And it is surprisingly strong too. Better than most lines I’ve run into.”

“’specially that green one,” Arbutus said, motioning to the frayed line.

“Ah,” the fish seemed to nod in recollection. “That line was actually my hardest battle. I was a little farther south, just off the coast, when I bit onto this line. I was shocked: there was no boat in sight, no predators nearby, nothing that made sense. This had to be someone who knew what they were doing, going for their biggest catch. And that was true. Within the first ten seconds I knew he was adept and using good equipment. But I had a couple escapes under my belt by that point and could think my way through this. Going out to sea was no good; he’d have enough line to tire me out. The bottom was too marshy, with nothing to catch on. I’d have to get in close, to let him think he was winning, and then outlast him somehow.

“The battle was a near-standstill for about twenty minutes. Eventually, as I got closer, I spotted the pillars of a pier. I tried baiting him into releasing some slack so I could spin around a couple pillars, but he kept it tight throughout. I’d only be able to get one pillar, and probably one of the ones closest to the sea. I took a chance and spun around one pillar before he had a chance to recover. There was a bolt sticking out, and I had just enough line to spin around it. It was a tough angle for both of us—him trying to wind me in and me trying to saw off the line. But I had the edge. The bolt cut strand by strand, and eventually all the way through. That’s why this line is the way you see today, very frayed.”

“Would you say that dent’l floss was harder to break than most lines?” asked Lester calmly.

Arbutus’ head swiveled around. “Now you’re interested?”

“I’m interested in anythin that works.” said Lester.

“Well, I didn’t break the dental floss,” said Howarth, “but yes, I think most of us would agree. It’s harder to see but also bends better.”

“How’s it versus that other heavy line?”

Howarth sighed, clearly bored with this line of questioning. “I don’t know. That one broke more easily, but that was because I had to break it. I’ve never fought as hard to break a line as I did that one.”

“But I thought you said that last one was the hardest fight?”

“It was, but that was just a personal achievement. This line happened during a time when food was scarce. Our school was going hungry, and fish were getting caught left and right. We needed to find food for our young, but we also couldn’t afford to lose more of our own. I went out because I had the best chance to escape a bad situation. Which of course I got into. The battle wasn’t as good—he wasn’t as skilled as The Green Line—but I fought hard because I had just found food for the school and needed to get back to them.”

“What kind of food do you like?”

Arbutus’ shoulders joined his head in swiveling, this time with some anger. “That’s downright rude what yer doin to our guest.”

“Oh, the usual things,” said Howarth. “Bugs and seaweed and crumbs. Occasionally a shrimp or two.”

“Any other crustaceans?” asked Lester?

“Crustaceans?”

“Shellfish. Shrimp, crabs, lobsters…seafood with shells.”

“We do eat them from time to time, yes.”

“We or you?”

“I have eaten them, yes.”

“Do they taste better when you eat them alive?”

Arbutus stood straight up. “You better stop right there, Lester Stick.”

“Oh come on, Booty Boy,” said Lester, rolling his head. “He’s a grouper. This is what groupers eat. This is how the world works. The predators win. You eat what you kill.”

“But you ain’t killed ‘im. He’s still there talking,” Arbutus said determinedly.

“Because you wanted to talk to ‘im for some reason.”

“Because he talked to us first!”

Lester said nothing.

“Besides, he’s my fish. I’m the one who caught ‘im.”

“But we always split up everything we catch when we get back.”

“Well we ain’t back yet. And you didn’t split that albacore with me when you caught that four years ago.”

“Oh, Arbutus, that was the COVID. We were all hurtin then.”

“I don’t see what that has to do with it. If I catch it, I get to do with it what makes sense to me.”

“And where’s that gotten you?”

Arbutus stared at his friend. Lester would make quips about his intelligence, but none of them ever felt as barbed as this one. He wasn’t sure of how to respond, whether to react to the quip, whether to retort that Lester had chosen to come with him, whether to point out that this was the first breach of fish-sharing etiquette between them, or just to slap his friend as he felt the urge to do. Lester glanced up at him for a second, then turned back.

“I’m gonna hear the story about the last line,” said Arbutus icily, and sat down.

Howarth nodded and began. “The wire and swivel is the only one of these I didn’t break. I collected it about four seasons ago. I was swimming in a school along with our leader. The line bobbed right in front of him, and he was about to bite it. I didn’t have time to stop him, just to muscle in instead and bite it. I realized right away that it was even stronger than I had expected and that I would not be able to break out of it. I stopped fighting and simply went up. I had done my job. I had saved our leader. I had lived a good life.

“I think the fisherman knew something was wrong when I went up easy, especially when he saw all the other lines in me. He looked at me in the same way you did, then cut the line and tossed me back in. No picture, no measurement. Just mercy.”

“Your sacrifice was accepted,” said Arbutus quietly. “It was returned to you in turn.”

“Yes. It was strange to receive it. I’ve always been the strong fish, the one to provide or to break lines for sport. To purposefully lose and then win because of it was different. No other fish in our school has ever broken a wire and swivel. And then there I am, reborn into the water, with a unique gift. Fish tell me I’ve changed a lot these last four seasons, and that’s why. I gave my life for another only to find it given back to me.”

“Rev’rend Tom always says that sacrifice remakes the world,” said Arbutus. “You can hold onto water all you want, but it’s always gonna slip through your fingers. Best you can do is realize that things ain’t yours and be generous with them.”

Lester could not hold his harrumph any longer and shifted so his friend and his friend’s aquatic mentor were out of view. But within seconds, he was standing. A quiver at the end of his line was followed by a whip and the telltale flex of the rod. He yelped with glee, grabbed his rod with a second hand, and prepared for battle. Arbutus jumped up and came alongside him, eager to see what his friend would land. It didn’t take long for Lester to reel in the fish and scoop him into the boat. The redfish was wriggling wildly, trying to flop its way out and gasping for air. Lester hugged it, trying to control the fish as its energy slowly died out. It was a good catch: about 28 inches long, with good color. Lester had caught a young fish and wrestled it for all it was worth. He let out a whoop and high-fived Arbutus before bending over the fish and pulling out his pliers to remove the hook.

“Dump the cooler and bring it over here, will ya?”

“Like dump all the water out?”

“Yeah, silly, it’s for our fish.”

“But Mister Howarth’s in there.”

“Call im Howarth all you want; he’s still dinner.”

Arbutus glared at his friend, who had not looked up the entire time they’d been talking. “I wasn’t done talkin with im.”

“You talked to ‘im for over an hour. It’s late. You got yers and I got mine. Don’t make me have to take care of yers.”

Arbutus, who was never given to great decisions, chose to make the best of a bad set of options. Walking over to the engine, he grabbed the throttle and sat down. “I’ve ‘ad enough. Time to go home.”

Lester was thrown sideways as the boat jumped to life, having been so focused on his fish that he hadn’t noticed his friend move. “The hell are you doing?” he yelled to a stone-face Arbutus. Although the sooner they could be back on land, the less he’d have to deal with this crazy fish and now-crazy friend.

As they re-entered cell range, Lester’s phone began ringing and ringing.

“Aren’t you gonna pick that up?” asked Arbutus.

“Nah. That’s Mr. Derek.”

“Must be serious if he’s callin you on yer day off. You sure you don’ need to talk?”

“Nah, he’s just gonna ask me where I am.”

Everyone was thrown again as Arbutus’ hand left the throttle. “Why’s he askin you where you are if it was yer day off?”

Lester was silent.

“Lester Stick, I’m not sure I rec’gnize you right now. Yer always tellin me that you know better than me, but I know that skippin out on Mister Derek to go fishin ain’t gonna keep you a job. Anyone knows that.”

“Anyone knows that?? Anyone? Does anyone know where Mister Derek keeps his boat keys? Does anyone know how Mister Derek actually makes his money? Does anyone know all the government people on his payroll? Does anyone know how leverage works? I do, which is why I knew the boat would be available today for us both. Or did you want to keep existin on church charity instead of findin a job to put food on the table?”

“A job like that?”

“Yes, like that!” Lester was frothing now. “Don’t lecture me on what’s right, Booty Boy. I know how the world works. You gotta take what you can get, any time. And if yer not gonna take that fish home for dinner, then I will.”

Lester turned for the cooler but Arbutus lunged, diving at his arm. Lester grabbed the cooler’s handle and tugged it as Arbutus tried to break his grasp. Lester was stronger, but Arbutus was focusing all his effort on Lester’s fingers, and it was working. The cooler sloshed around, spilling an increasing amount of water in the boat. Lester yanked it harder, and the cooler banged into the first seat, kicking it into the air. The lid opened, tossing Howarth and the rest of the water upwards and out of the boat.

“No!” cried Lester, trying to reach for Howarth, who was in the water observing the scene. Lester pushed off, trying to gain leverage, but his foot started on the redfish and slipped immediately, shooting the redfish behind him and out the side of the boat. Lester screamed. “You idiot! Look what you’ve done! All the work we did for nothing.”

Arbutus peeked his head over the rail, looking at Howarth. The two creatures regarded each other, then Howarth disappeared from view below the surface.

By the time they got back it was dusk. Neither had talked after the fight, and they barely chatted as they loaded the boat and drove back. Arbutus usually followed Lester in prioritizing fishing over church on Sundays, but this time he didn’t show up and Lester didn’t bother calling him.

Lester took the boat out again at dawn, stopping past The Hole and casting his line down on one side. Nothing. He tried the other side. Nothing. After ten hours on the water, the only thing he had caught was sunburn, and he returned an irritable man. Waiting for him on the dock was Arbutus. The two friends stood silently, barely able to look at each other. Finally Arbutus spoke.

“I was convict’d bout fishin on Sunday. Don’t know why, but knew I couldn do it. I figured you’d understand. Figured you might want the quiet too.”

“It was different.”

Arbutus held out a paper bag. “Angela baked a bunch of muffins this afternoon. I thought they might be nice with the fish you caught.”

Lester looked at the bag, then at Arbutus, and grew incensed. Snatching the bag, he ripped it open. “This!? This is s’pposed to make up for that grouper!?” He grabbed a handful of muffins and threw it on the water. “This is s’pposed to make up for the redfish?” Another handful. “This is s’pposed to go along with the zero fish that I caught??” Another handful. “Why don’t you just open your own dental floss fishing school and charge admission?” He reached in but the bag was empty.

Arbutus stood confused, sorrowful, and silent, but then his eyes got wide. “Look!” he said, pointing behind Lester.

Lester turned but didn’t see anything. “What.”

“The fish! The fish are back!” He ran past Lester, almost knocking him off the dock. “Don’t you see all the groupers? Don’t you see them all eating the muffins? Boy, they’re big’uns. And look! There’s Mister Howarth!”

Lester could not see any such fish, but he grabbed his line instinctively. Forming the last bits of muffin into a ball, he shoved it on the end of the hook and cast it towards where Arbutus was pointing.

“What are you doing?” cried Arbutus.

Lester was stone-faced. “I’m getting my share.”

The hook fell about five yards behind the fish. All save one retreated below the surface and away. Howarth remained, head poked above water just like in the cooler, looking at Arbutus, who frantically tried to shoo him away. As Lester reeled the hook towards Howarth, the fish disappeared from view. Lester stopped, leaving the hook where the fish had been, but no bite. Arbutus relaxed, then Lester. The line snapped taut in a moment. The reel nearly yanked out of Lester’s hands, but he managed to hold on, which dragged him right off the dock and into the water. Arbutus dropped down and grabbed Lester’s ankles, but Lester was locked onto the fish. Howarth was diving, going below the surface. Lester was trying to reel him in, but he was going lower and lower. Taking a deep breath, Lester ducked under the surface. It was working. The rod wasn’t flexing as much and the fish felt like it was coming closer. Then suddenly the flex stopped. There was still tension, so it hadn’t escaped. The fish was getting closer in a hurry. Lester peered through the murky water. Nothing. Then all at once, a shape, then a fish, then a giant tail smacking him across the face as it zoomed past.

Lester gasped in shock, causing him to choke on the water he inhaled. He tried to keep holding the rod, but he was coughing too hard to keep focus. The rod slipped, tugged by the grouper, vanishing out of his hands and back into the darkness. Arbutus managed to grab Lester’s belt and drag him up to the dock level. Lester rested there, propped halfway out of the water like the fish he’d been chasing. Arbutus collapsed backwards and sat crab-legged on the dock. Both men gasped for breath.

The fish had poked his head above the water and watched the two men from afar. Arbutus looked up in time and saw him, but the fish vanished below the surface just as he was about to say something. Arbutus closed his mouth, for once in his life thinking better than to say the first thing on his mind. He glanced over at Lester, but Lester was still gasping. The fish had escaped again.

Arbutus walked back to the marina where Angela had dropped him off and sat down outside. A man came through the door staggeringly trying to carry a cooler. Arbutus jumped up and ran over to him.

“Can I help you Mister?”

“Oh, thank you so much,” said the older man. “Here, you grab one side and I’ll grab the other. My truck’s right over there.”

They carried over the cooler and got it loaded in the back. “What a day,” said the man. “Never seen the fish bite anything like it at the end. Caught four small ones in the first six hours, then two big ones in the last hour. You couldn’t have caught more if you were walking on water.” He paused for a second. “Here, you take the big one.”

“Oh, no, I couldn’t,” protested Arbutus.

“I insist,” said the man, reaching for the cooler. “I wouldn’t have gotten any back here if you hadn’t helped.” He pulled out a snapper and handed it to Arbutus. “Thanks, friend.”

“Thank you, mister. This is quite the fish. Bout forty inches?”

“Dead on.” The man smiled and opened the car door. “I’ll always have the story. They all have to get away sometime.” He pulled away, leaving Arbutus with the prize fish.

Arbutus admired the fish as he walked back to the bench. There weren’t any barnacles or hooks on this one. No stories to be told. He sat down to wait for Angela, but his curiosity got the better of him before long. He leaned over the snapper and cautiously whispered, “Have you ever met Mister Howarth?”
